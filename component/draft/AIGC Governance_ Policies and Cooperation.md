# [C10] §4.1 Government Policies and International Cooperation on AIGC Governance

The rapid proliferation and increasing sophistication of Artificial Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC) have catalyzed a profound paradigm shift in global technology governance. As generative models demonstrate unprecedented capabilities in text, image, audio, and code generation, sovereign states and supranational entities have mobilized to architect regulatory frameworks capable of mitigating systemic risks—ranging from electoral manipulation and financial fraud to the erosion of digital trust—without stifling technological innovation. The regulatory landscape of 2025 and 2026 is characterized by distinct, often competing, jurisdictional approaches that reflect divergent political philosophies. This section provides an exhaustive examination of the institutional mechanisms, legislative architectures, and technical standards governing AIGC across the European Union, the United States, and China, culminating in an analysis of the multilateral efforts attempting to harmonize these disparate regimes.

## **1\. The European Union: Prescriptive Harmonization and Fundamental Rights**

The European Union operates under a normative doctrine that prioritizes fundamental human rights, democratic integrity, and consumer protection. Its approach to AIGC governance is highly prescriptive, horizontal, and ex-ante, establishing a rigorous compliance architecture designed to create a "Brussels Effect" that exports European regulatory standards to the global market.

## **1.1 The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) and the Risk-Tiered Architecture**

At the core of the European regulatory apparatus is the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 of the European Parliament and of the Council, officially accessible via the EUR-Lex database at [https://eur-lex.europa.eu](https://eur-lex.europa.eu)), which formally entered into force on August 1, 2024\.1 The legislation abandons a monolithic regulatory approach in favor of a granular, risk-tiered framework that classifies artificial intelligence systems based on their intended use, their deployment context, and their potential to cause societal harm or infringe upon fundamental rights.3

This framework establishes four distinct tiers of regulatory scrutiny, each imposing asymmetric obligations on the developers (providers) and operators (deployers) of AI systems.

| Risk Tier | Regulatory Treatment and Obligations | Examples Relevant to AIGC |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Unacceptable Risk** | Banned entirely from the European single market. These systems contravene EU values. | AIGC systems deployed for subliminal manipulation, social scoring by state actors, or untargeted scraping of facial images to build or expand biometric recognition databases.1 |
| **High Risk** | Subject to strict ex-ante conformity assessments, mandatory fundamental rights impact assessments, rigorous risk management systems, and high-quality data governance. | AI systems used as safety components in critical infrastructure, recruitment algorithms (e.g., CV-sorting software), or educational scoring. While standalone GenAI models are rarely high-risk by default, AIGC engines integrated into these sensitive sectors elevate to this tier.1 |
| **Limited Risk** | Subject to specific transparency, disclosure, and labeling obligations to ensure users are aware they are interacting with an artificial entity. | Chatbots, deepfake generators, and general-purpose generative AI systems that interact directly with end-users or generate synthetic media.3 |
| **Minimal/No Risk** | Unregulated under the specific provisions of the AI Act, though still subject to broader EU laws such as the GDPR and consumer protection directives. | AI-enabled spam filters or basic video game mechanics that do not manipulate human behavior or generate deceptive public content.1 |

The foundational premise of this legislative architecture is that the regulatory burden should scale symmetrically with the potential for harm. For AIGC, the most profound implications reside within the Limited Risk tier, which mandates transparency to ensure that end-users remain cognizant of their interactions with synthetic entities and manipulated content.3

## **1.2 Article 50 Transparency Obligations and the Deepfake Mandate**

Article 50 of the AI Act establishes stringent transparency requirements specifically targeting the generation and manipulation of synthetic content. These obligations represent a critical mechanism for preserving the epistemological integrity of the European digital public sphere.2

Under Article 50(2), providers of generative AI systems must ensure that AI-generated or manipulated outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and are detectable as artificially generated.7 Concurrently, under Article 50(4), deployers—entities or individuals utilizing these systems—must clearly disclose when realistic synthetic content, commonly referred to as deepfakes, has been generated or manipulated.7 The legislation dictates that deepfakes must be labeled even when the underlying content is entirely lawful, operating on the principle that the deception regarding the content's origin is inherently harmful to public trust.7

The regulatory architecture makes a nuanced distinction for artistic, creative, satirical, or fictional works. In such instances, to protect freedom of expression and the arts, the disclosure requirements are reduced to a minimal, non-intrusive standard that does not impede the enjoyment or distribution of the work.7

Failure to comply with the AI Act's provisions carries severe financial repercussions. The enforcement mechanisms allow for administrative fines of up to €35 million or 7% of a company's total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year, whichever is higher, creating a formidable deterrent against non-compliance and forcing global technology firms to adapt their internal compliance structures to European standards.2

**Implementation Timeline:** The AI Act relies on a staggered implementation timeline to allow the market to adjust to its expansive requirements (detailed at [https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/implementation-timeline/](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/implementation-timeline/)).2

* **August 1, 2024:** The AI Act officially entered into force.2  
* **February 2, 2025:** Prohibitions on "Unacceptable Risk" AI systems and general AI literacy obligations became fully applicable.1  
* **August 2, 2025:** The transparency obligations under Article 50 (including deepfake labeling), the governance rules for General Purpose AI (GPAI) models, and the penalties framework become fully applicable and enforceable.1  
* **August 2, 2026:** The AI Act becomes fully applicable in its entirety.1  
* **August 2, 2027:** The rules for high-risk AI systems that are embedded into regulated products (e.g., medical devices, vehicles) enter into application following an extended transition period.1

The implementation of these provisions is overseen by the newly established European AI Office, situated within the European Commission. The AI Office acts as the central enforcement and coordination hub, facilitating the development of secondary legislation, drafting codes of practice, and ensuring harmonized application across the national competent authorities of the Member States.8

## **1.3 The Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content**

To operationalize the broad, technologically neutral mandates of Article 50, the European Commission, via the AI Office, facilitated the drafting of the voluntary Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content (details available via [https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content)).8 Drafted by independent experts and heavily influenced by stakeholder consultations involving hundreds of participants from industry, academia, civil society, and Member States, the Code serves as the practical compliance manual for the AI Act.8

The first draft, published on December 17, 2025, outlined expansive measures targeting two distinct groups: providers of generative AI systems and the deployers of those systems.11 Following an extensive public feedback period, a significantly streamlined second draft was published on March 5, 2026, aimed at reducing the compliance burden while maintaining technical feasibility.10

For **providers**, Section 1 of the revised Code mandates a multi-layered marking approach. This includes the embedding of secure provenance information into the metadata of the output (such as digital signatures utilizing open standards like C2PA) alongside the application of interwoven, imperceptible watermarks applied directly into the content at the pixel or audio-wave level.10 These watermarks must be robust enough to withstand typical digital processing steps, including compression, cropping, and screenshotting.12 Providers are also expected to offer free-of-charge detection interfaces (e.g., APIs) enabling third parties to verify the provenance of content.13

For **deployers**, particularly those utilizing AIGC for professional purposes or producing text on matters of public interest, Section 2 of the Code introduces the concept of a uniform, interactive "EU Icon".10 The 2026 draft proposed the creation of a dedicated task force to finalize the design of this interactive icon, which will be made freely available to signatories to serve as a standardized visual cue for European consumers.10 While the Code is technically voluntary, adherence to it serves as a formal presumption of conformity with the binding legal obligations of Article 50(2) and (4) of the AI Act, meaning that organizations opting out must independently prove their alternative methods are equally effective.8

## **1.4 Digital Services Act (DSA): Platform Intermediation and AIGC**

While the AI Act governs the *creation* and *deployment* of AIGC, the Digital Services Act (DSA) governs its *dissemination* across digital intermediaries. The DSA introduces asymmetric obligations on online platforms, scaling with their size, user base, and societal impact. Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) and Very Large Online Search Engines (VLOSEs)—defined as those reaching more than 45 million active monthly users in the EU—are subject to the most stringent requirements.14

Under the DSA, platforms are legally required to conduct comprehensive systemic risk assessments. In the context of 2025 and 2026, the European Commission has mandated that these assessments implicitly include the risks posed by the viral spread of illicit AIGC, such as non-consensual deepfake pornography, deepfake electoral disinformation, and AI-assisted financial fraud.14 The DSA complements the AI Act by mandating that platforms provide robust, user-friendly mechanisms for individuals to flag illegal content and establish internal complaint-handling systems.15

If a platform utilizes an integrated AIGC tool (such as an embedded AI chatbot or a native image generator), the Commission increasingly views these integrations as falling under the purview of VLOP systemic risk obligations. Consequently, platforms must proactively mitigate the risk of their native chatbots generating harmful instructions or aiding in the planning of violent attacks.14 Furthermore, the DSA's transparency requirements obligate platforms to explain their content moderation decisions and the parameters of their algorithmic recommender systems, ensuring that AI-generated misinformation is not artificially amplified.15 The interplay between the AI Act and the DSA represents a comprehensive, overlapping regulatory net: the AI Act ensures the content is marked at the point of generation, while the DSA ensures platforms detect these markers, honor the metadata, and moderate the content appropriately to mitigate systemic harms.16

## **1.5 GDPR Implications for AI Training Data**

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) continues to exert profound influence over the foundational stages of AIGC development, specifically regarding the scraping and processing of model training data. The fundamental tension lies between the GDPR's data minimization principles and individual rights frameworks, and the voracious, indiscriminate data requirements of Large Language Models (LLMs) and diffusion models.

A critical friction point is the Right to Erasure (Article 17). When an individual requests the deletion of their personal data, AI developers face significant technical hurdles if that data has already been ingested into the weights of a trained neural network. In late 2025 and early 2026, the French Data Protection Authority (CNIL) and the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) issued highly anticipated guidance on this dilemma (accessible via [https://www.edpb.europa.eu](https://www.edpb.europa.eu) and [https://www.cnil.fr](https://www.cnil.fr)).17

The guidance stipulates that if an organization can verify that personal data has been explicitly memorized by an AI model and can be regurgitated in prompts, they must inform the individual and facilitate its removal.17 However, acknowledging the boundaries of current computer science, regulators have conceded that the right to erasure in trained AI models is not absolute. Organizations must conduct a balancing test, weighing the sensitivity of the data and the negative impact on the individual against the technical feasibility and exorbitant computational cost of retraining an entire foundational model from scratch.18

Furthermore, the deployment of GenAI systems fundamentally requires rigorous Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs).20 Organizations must justify their legal basis for scraping publicly available personal data for training, moving away from broad, legally precarious claims of "legitimate interest" toward more strictly defined, privacy-preserving techniques. If an AI model is found to be developed with unlawfully processed personal data, the EDPB has warned that this could impact the lawfulness of the model's entire deployment, potentially resulting in orders to destroy the algorithm unless the model can be retroactively and duly anonymized.18

## **2\. The United States: Volatility, Federalism, and Market-Driven Paradigms**

In stark contrast to the European Union's centralized, ex-ante harmonization, the United States presents a highly fragmented and dynamic governance landscape. US policy is characterized by volatile shifts at the federal executive level, a heavy reliance on voluntary technical standards, legislative gridlock in the federal Congress, and an aggressive assumption of regulatory authority by individual state legislatures seeking to fill the void.

## **2.1 Federal Executive Volatility: From Biden to Trump (2023–2026)**

The federal approach to AIGC underwent a seismic ideological and administrative reversal between 2023 and 2026, highlighting the vulnerabilities of governing emerging technology purely through executive action.

In October 2023, the Biden administration issued Executive Order 14110 on "Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence".21 This directive represented a comprehensive attempt to impose safety reporting requirements on frontier AI developers utilizing the Defense Production Act.21 Crucially for AIGC, EO 14110 directed the Department of Commerce to develop rigorous testing standards for synthetic content, including watermarking technologies, and mandated that agencies assess the risks of AI-generated misinformation.21

This regulatory paradigm was abruptly dismantled following the inauguration of President Donald Trump. On January 23, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order 14179 titled "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence," which explicitly revoked Biden's EO 14110 (published in the Federal Register, [https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/removing-barriers-to-american-leadership-in-artificial-intelligence/](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/removing-barriers-to-american-leadership-in-artificial-intelligence/)).23 The new administration characterized the previous framework as imposing "onerous and unnecessary government control" that hindered private sector innovation and threatened American global technological dominance.22 The Trump EO mandated an immediate review and rescission of all agency actions taken under the Biden order, instructing agency heads to provide all available exemptions to developers in the interim to prevent any slowdown in AI development.23

This ideological pivot culminated in the release of "America's AI Action Plan" in July 2025\.25 The Action Plan shifted the federal focus entirely away from mandatory safety testing and toward deregulation, massive infrastructure expansion (specifically regarding data centers, semiconductor manufacturing, and energy procurement on federal lands), and the aggressive promotion of American AI models abroad.25 Crucially regarding AIGC, the Trump administration emphasized "ideological neutrality" in Large Language Models, establishing a policy to ensure AI systems are "free from ideological bias or engineered social agendas," reflecting a distinct domestic political priority over technical safety parameters.23

## **2.2 Voluntary Standards: The NIST AI Risk Management Framework**

Despite the political volatility at the White House, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) remains the preeminent federal authority on technical AI governance and standards. Operating outside the realm of binding legislation, NIST relies on voluntary adherence to its frameworks by the private sector, aligning with the US preference for market-driven compliance.

NIST's foundational document, the AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, accessible at [https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)), was expanded to address the unique complexities of AIGC through the release of the "Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile" (NIST-AI-600-1) in mid-2024.28 The Generative AI Profile identifies risks specific to foundation models—such as hallucinations, deepfake generation, the facilitation of biological weapons design, and the difficulty of attributing provenance—and maps them to actionable, voluntary mitigation strategies for both developers and downstream deployers.28

Building on this, in December 2025, NIST published a preliminary draft of the "Cybersecurity Framework Profile for Artificial Intelligence" (Cyber AI Profile, NIST IR 8596).31 This specialized framework integrates AI risks into the broader NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0, establishing a three-pillar focus area:

1. **Secure:** Securing AI systems from adversarial attacks (such as prompt injection, data poisoning, and model inversion).  
2. **Detect:** Utilizing AI to enhance conventional cybersecurity threat detection.  
3. **Thwart:** Defending organizational networks against the adversarial use of generative AI by malicious actors conducting automated phishing or vulnerability discovery.32

## **2.3 State-Level Divergence: The Vanguard of US Regulation**

The absence of comprehensive federal legislation has created a profound regulatory vacuum that individual states have moved aggressively to fill. This de facto technological federalism has resulted in a patchwork of regulations that force national AI developers to navigate overlapping, and sometimes contradictory, compliance requirements.

| State | Key Legislation & Status | Focus and Scope Regarding AIGC |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **California** | **SB 53** (Transparency in Frontier AI Act) \- Effective Jan 1, 2026\. **AB 2655** (Defending Democracy from Deepfake Deception Act). | SB 53 is the first US law directly regulating the developers of massive "frontier" foundation models. Passed as a pared-down successor to the vetoed SB 1047, it requires developers to publicly disclose protocols for mitigating catastrophic risks and establish mandatory incident reporting to state regulators.33 AB 2655 aggressively targets election deepfakes, requiring large online platforms to block or label materially deceptive content related to elections. |
| **Texas** | **TRAIGA** (Responsible AI Governance Act) \- Effective Jan 1, 2026\. | Prohibits the development or deployment of AIGC for behavioral manipulation, algorithmic discrimination, or the creation of non-consensual sexually explicit deepfakes. It establishes a state AI Advisory Council and a regulatory sandbox, focusing heavily on targeted prohibitions rather than broad model transparency like California.36 |
| **New York** | Assorted Deepfake & AI Policies. | Operates a matrix of laws addressing the downstream use of AIGC. Requires strict disclosures for political deepfakes utilized prior to elections. New York laws hold broadcasters (e.g., radio stations) liable if they air undisclosed AI-generated political ads.40 |
| **Colorado** | **SB 205** (Colorado AI Act) \- Implementation delayed. | Originally slated as a broad consumer protection bill imposing anti-discrimination requirements on high-risk AI, implementation was delayed in 2025 due to industry pushback and the complexity of localized enforcement.42 |

California's approach under SB 53 represents a significant structural shift in US governance. Rather than regulating the downstream *application* of AI (as seen in Texas), California has targeted the underlying *development* of frontier models trained on massive computational resources (greater than 10^26 floating-point operations).35 This creates a domestic "California Effect," where national tech giants must conform their fundamental model architectures and safety protocols to California state law simply to operate within the state, effectively setting a baseline standard for the entire country.35

## **2.4 Congressional Stagnation and Targeted Legislative Efforts**

At the federal legislative level, the US Congress has repeatedly failed to pass comprehensive, omnibus AI legislation akin to the EU AI Act. Instead, Congressional efforts in 2025 and 2026 have focused narrowly on highly visible, politically salient harms associated with AIGC, primarily fraud, deepfake pornography, and electoral interference.

The **DEFIANCE Act** (Disrupt Explicit Forged Images and Non-Consensual Edits Act, H.R. 3562\) gained significant bipartisan momentum in 2026\. The legislation establishes a federal civil right of action, allowing victims of non-consensual intimate deepfakes to sue the creators and distributors of the content for monetary damages and injunctive relief.43 This civil approach supplements the earlier **Take It Down Act**, enacted in May 2025, which criminalized the creation of digital forgeries and mandated that platforms implement notice-and-removal procedures within 48 hours.44

Addressing financial exploitation, the **AI Fraud Deterrence Act** (H.R. 6306, introduced in late 2025\) targets the use of AIGC to impersonate individuals—including senior government officials and private citizens—for financial gain. The Act enhances penalties for mail and wire fraud committed with the assistance of artificial intelligence, doubling maximum fines to $2,000,000.46

Concurrently, the proposed **SCAM Act** (Safeguarding Consumers from Advertising Misconduct, introduced February 2026\) attempts to thread the needle of Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act. It seeks to hold social media platforms legally responsible for verifying the identity of advertisers and stopping fraudulent, AI-generated scam ads before they go live, regulating the commercial transaction of the ad rather than the user-generated speech directly.47 However, comprehensive legislation regulating political deepfakes at the federal level, such as the **REAL Political Ads Act**, has struggled to overcome First Amendment constitutional scrutiny, leaving the critical issue of election security primarily to the fractured landscape of state-level regulations.48

## **3\. China: State Control, Ideological Alignment, and Application-Specific Governance**

The People's Republic of China has engineered the world's most rapid, agile, and centrally administered regulatory framework for AIGC. Driven by a dual mandate to dominate the global AI market while maintaining absolute sovereign control over the domestic information ecosystem, China's approach relies on iterative, application-specific regulations governed by a strict ex-ante filing system.

## **3.1 The Phased Regulatory Architecture**

Rather than attempting to regulate artificial intelligence through a single comprehensive law that takes years to draft, the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) deployed a phased regulatory strategy targeting specific technological modalities exactly as they emerged into the consumer market.

The foundation was laid with the **Deep Synthesis Management Provisions**, which became effective on January 10, 2023\.49 This regulation explicitly targeted technologies utilizing deep learning to generate text, images, audio, or video. It required service providers to implement stringent content management systems, verify the real-name identity of all users to eliminate anonymity, and affix prominent labels to synthetic content to prevent public deception.49

As Large Language Models rapidly advanced, China introduced the **Interim Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services**, effective August 15, 2023\.50 The Interim Measures attempt to balance the state's desire for technological innovation with severe security imperatives. Crucially, while the regulations grant exemptions to models used purely for internal research and development, any AIGC service offered to the public must comply with exhaustive mandates.51 Providers are held legally responsible for the outputs of their models, must ensure the legality and legitimacy of their training data, and are required to actively monitor and filter content to ensure it upholds "core socialist values".50

## **3.2 The Algorithm Filing System: Mechanisms of Ex-Ante Control**

The most distinct and powerful feature of China's AIGC governance is the Algorithm Filing System. Under the Internet Information Service Algorithmic Recommendation Management Provisions, any AI service provider whose technology possesses "public opinion attributes or social mobilization capabilities" must conduct a rigorous internal risk assessment and formally file their algorithm with the CAC before it can be deployed to the public.51

Because almost all generative AI applications—from conversational chatbots to text-to-image generators—are deemed by regulators to possess "social mobilization capabilities," the filing system operates as a mandatory, ex-ante licensing regime.52 The CAC subjects the submitted algorithms to rigorous security assessments, reviewing the source of the training data, the efficacy of the moderation filters, and the mechanisms used to prevent the generation of prohibited political content. By the end of 2025, China had approved thousands of algorithm filings, demonstrating a highly active, heavily bureaucratized gateway to market entry that grants the state total visibility into the commercial AI landscape.51

## **3.3 TC260: The 31 Security Risks Framework and Corpus Safety**

The technical bedrock supporting China's legislative mandates is provided by the National Information Security Standardization Technical Committee (TC260). In early 2024, TC260 finalized "Basic Security Requirements for Generative AI Services" (TC260-003, full translation available via Georgetown CSET), a comprehensive technical document outlining the exact security risks that developers must demonstrably mitigate.53

The TC260 framework is uniquely demanding in its focus on five core categories, with the most critical being **"corpus safety"** (training data integrity).54 It explicitly outlines 31 specific security risks, categorizing them into universal concerns—such as algorithmic bias, copyright infringement, and data privacy—and China-specific political imperatives.54 AIGC providers must employ rigorous oversight processes to ensure their models do not generate content that subverts state power, violates censorship laws, or answers questions regarding Chinese history and politics in a manner inconsistent with official state doctrine.54

Furthermore, the framework imposes strict supply chain security requirements under its "safety measures" category, demanding that AI service providers divide their training and inference environments to prevent data leakage, establish continuous monitoring to thwart input attacks (jailbreaking), and conduct security assessments of the underlying hardware and software infrastructure powering their models.54 To legally claim compliance and pass the CAC filing, developers must statistically demonstrate that a highly specific percentage of their model's generated samples do not contain any of the 31 enumerated risks, fundamentally intertwining statistical machine learning evaluation with state censorship.57

## **3.4 Practical Enforcement and Judicial Precedents**

China's regulatory apparatus is not merely theoretical; it is actively and forcefully enforced. Throughout 2025 and 2026, the CAC and the Chinese judiciary demonstrated a willingness to strictly penalize non-compliance while simultaneously recognizing the commercial value of AIGC intellectual property.

In late 2025, the Shanghai branch of the CAC initiated the "Sword Pujiang" special enforcement action, conducting "look-back" inspections on generative AI websites. The administration ordered domestic app stores to delist 54 applications that utilized generative AI without proper algorithm filing or security assessments.58 The CAC also leveraged the Interim Measures to officially file cases and impose financial and operational penalties against service providers that refused to rectify their deepfake labeling protocols or unlawfully provided "paper ghostwriting" functions that bypassed state educational controls.58

Simultaneously, the Chinese judicial system has begun to establish firm precedents regarding AIGC intellectual property and liability. In landmark cases in 2024 and 2025, courts in Hangzhou and Wuhan ruled that certain images generated with AI possess sufficient human originality in the prompting and iterative refinement process to be eligible for copyright protection, a stark contrast to the US Copyright Office's general refusal to grant such protections.59 Conversely, the Hangzhou Internet Court issued strict judgments holding generative AI platforms directly liable for contributory copyright infringement when their models replicated protected works without authorization, establishing a dual framework that protects AIGC outputs while severely punishing the unauthorized scraping of training data.59

## **4\. International Cooperation: The Pursuit of Multilateral Alignment**

The transboundary nature of generative AI renders purely domestic regulation inherently insufficient. In response, 2025 and 2026 witnessed an unprecedented acceleration in multilateral diplomacy, as nations sought to forge consensus on AIGC safety evaluation, technical standard-setting, and equitable global adoption.

## **4.1 Institutional Evaluation: The UK AI Security Institute**

A cornerstone of the international effort has been the institutionalization of state-backed AI evaluation capabilities, led prominently by the United Kingdom. Originally established as the AI Safety Institute following the 2023 Bletchley Park Summit, the organization was notably rebranded in early 2025 as the **AI Security Institute (AISI)** by the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology. This rebranding reflected a hardened mandate focusing on national security, critical infrastructure protection, and defense against AI-enabled cybercrime.61

The AISI operates with a mandate to empirically evaluate the capabilities and risks of frontier AI models prior to and post-deployment. The Institute's work culminated in the publication of the UK government's first "Frontier AI Trends Report" in December 2025\.63 The report provided critical, data-driven insights, noting that AI performance in sensitive domains is doubling every eight months.63 Crucially, the AISI documented that frontier models have surpassed PhD-level human baselines in chemistry and biology by up to 60%, and can now complete highly sophisticated, expert-level cyber tasks unassisted.63

Beyond internal evaluation, the AISI serves as an international hub for methodological standardization. It has open-sourced its evaluation toolkits—such as InspectCyber and ControlArena—enabling partner nations, academics, and developers to conduct standardized stress-tests for agentic behavior, self-replication, and "sandbagging" (where a model strategically hides its capabilities during evaluation).64

## **4.2 The International AI Safety Reports (2025–2026)**

Commissioned as a result of the Bletchley Summit, the "International AI Safety Report" series, chaired by Turing Award laureate Yoshua Bengio, provides the most authoritative scientific consensus on global AIGC risks.

The 2025 and 2026 editions synthesized the views of over 100 experts from 30 nations, highlighting a critical, widening gap between the exponential pace of AI capabilities and the sluggish development of governance frameworks.65 The 2026 report specifically narrowed its focus to "emerging risks," concluding that the empirical evidence for real-world harm is currently strongest in the domain of cybersecurity. The report documented a dramatic 92.7% year-over-year increase in data exfiltration volumes and a sharp rise in generative AI-assisted phishing, social engineering, and identity-based attacks.68

Furthermore, the Bengio reports highlighted profound deficiencies in current industry self-regulation. The 2025 AI Safety Index survey revealed that major tech firms remain fundamentally unprepared for their own stated goals of achieving Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), with none scoring above a 'D' in existential safety planning.69 The reports noted that existing safety evaluations are highly vulnerable to models "gaming" the tests by distinguishing between evaluation and deployment contexts, establishing that reliable pre-deployment safety testing has become exceedingly difficult to conduct.66 Notably, the United States declined to endorse the 2026 report, signaling a symbolic retreat from formal international safety consensus under the newly installed Trump administration.70

## **4.3 The Summit Trajectory: From Bletchley to New Delhi**

The multilateral dialogue on AI governance has been structured around an ongoing series of high-level global summits. By analyzing the progression of these summits, a clear evolution in the global policy narrative emerges: moving from an initial panic regarding existential safety, toward an emphasis on economic adoption, sustainability, and sovereign empowerment.

| Summit & Date | Key Agreements and Thematic Focus | Relevance to AIGC Governance |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Bletchley Park (Nov 2023\)** | The Bletchley Declaration | Initiated the global dialogue. Focused almost exclusively on catastrophic "frontier" safety risks, loss of control, and established the mandate for the International AI Safety Reports.67 |
| **Seoul (May 2024\)** | Seoul Declaration & Ministerial Statement | Broadened the scope to include "Innovation and Inclusivity." Yielded the "Frontier AI Safety Commitments" where 16 tech companies voluntarily pledged to increase transparency, conduct red-teaming, and publish safety frameworks for advanced models.73 |
| **Paris (Feb 2025\)** | AI Action Summit Declaration | Marked a distinct narrative shift toward optimism, economic adoption, and environmental sustainability. Launched a $400M public-interest AI fund and a coalition to address the massive energy footprint of AIGC data centers. The US and UK notably refused to sign the final declaration.76 |
| **India / New Delhi (Feb 2026\)** | New Delhi Declaration on AI Impact | Shifted the agenda toward the Global South. Focused on the "Democratization of AI," ensuring open-source access, and building sovereign capabilities. Established the "Trusted AI Commons" repository to share benchmarks and open frameworks globally.68 |

This trajectory illustrates a fracturing in global priorities. While early summits fostered unified concern over deepfakes, biological terrorism, and model alignment, subsequent summits (Paris and India) revealed a divergence. Developing nations prioritized open-source access and economic growth, while Western nations debated the optimal balance of regulation versus innovation, culminating in the US withdrawing from the Paris consensus to protect its domestic industry from frameworks it deemed overly restrictive.77

## **4.4 The G7 Hiroshima AI Process and OECD Principles**

Parallel to the summit series, established multilateral economic institutions have developed critical soft-law frameworks to guide organizational behavior across borders.

Under the Japanese presidency, the G7 launched the **Hiroshima AI Process**, culminating in the "International Code of Conduct for Organizations Developing Advanced AI Systems" (document available via [https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/hiroshima-process-international-code-conduct-advanced-ai-systems](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/hiroshima-process-international-code-conduct-advanced-ai-systems)).82 This voluntary Code explicitly targets the developers of the most advanced foundation models and generative AI systems. It establishes a lifecycle approach, urging organizations to conduct precautionary pre-deployment risk assessments, mitigate vulnerabilities post-deployment, and facilitate responsible information sharing regarding security incidents.84 Crucially, the Code contains specific provisions regarding AIGC, urging developers to implement content authentication mechanisms (like watermarks), manage training data quality to prevent bias, and respect intellectual property rights.86 To operationalize this, the OECD launched a global reporting framework in 2025, allowing major tech firms (including Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic) to publicly log their compliance with the Hiroshima Code, fostering an ecosystem of transparency and accountability.88

Concurrently, the **OECD AI Principles**—the foundational intergovernmental standard on AI originally adopted in 2019 (accessible at [https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles))—were significantly updated in May 2024 to address the unique disruptions caused by generative AI.89 The 2024 revisions explicitly elevated the importance of "information integrity," directly addressing the threats of deepfakes and mass disinformation.90 The updated principles maintain their core values of inclusive growth, human rights, transparency, and accountability, while adding specific directives for policymakers to address safety concerns by ensuring models can be overridden or safely decommissioned by human interaction if they exhibit undesired or dangerous behaviors.89

## **4.5 Comparative Analysis: A Tripartite World Order**

The global governance of AIGC has definitively fractured into three distinct paradigms, each reflecting the underlying political and economic philosophies of its architect.

The **European Union** model is profoundly prescriptive, rights-centric, and rule-based. By relying on heavy ex-ante conformity assessments and stringent transparency mandates (such as mandatory deepfake labeling, metadata embedding, and pixel-level watermarking), the EU seeks to eliminate technological risk before it reaches the consumer. The strength of this approach lies in providing unparalleled protection for democratic institutions, copyright holders, and individual privacy. However, its profound weakness is its immense bureaucratic friction. Industry critics argue that the sheer cost of compliance stifles domestic innovation, relegating Europe to the role of a regulatory superpower that imports foundational models rather than an economic superpower that builds them.

The **United States** model is fundamentally voluntary, market-driven, and principle-based, characterized by intense federal volatility and deferred regulatory action. At the federal level, the US relies on market forces and voluntary NIST frameworks to guide development, explicitly prioritizing technological dominance, deregulation, and rapid commercialization as seen in the 2025 AI Action Plan. The strength of this approach is its facilitation of unparalleled innovation, computational scale, and capital investment. The severe weakness, however, is the resultant regulatory vacuum. This has forced individual states like California and Texas to enact a patchwork of uncoordinated laws, creating compliance chaos for developers and offering US citizens wildly inconsistent legal protections against AIGC harms like non-consensual deepfakes, algorithmic bias, and financial fraud.

The **China** model is centrally administered, application-specific, and deeply entwined with state security. Utilizing mechanisms like the CAC Algorithm Filing System and TC260's rigid "corpus safety" requirements, China subjects AIGC to continuous, rigorous state oversight. The strength of this model is its unparalleled agility; the Chinese state can respond to new technological modalities with astonishing speed, rapidly excising harmful applications from the market and mandating strict identity verification to eliminate digital anonymity. The critical weakness is its inherent subordination of technological potential to ideological control. By forcing developers into an exhaustively rigid censorship framework that mandates adherence to official state narratives, the state risks structurally handicapping the semantic breadth, creative utility, and global exportability of Chinese foundational models.

Ultimately, international efforts—from the UK's AISI evaluations and the Bengio reports to the OECD principles and the evolving Summit declarations—represent a desperate diplomatic attempt to bridge these three irreconcilable philosophies. However, as the transboundary capabilities of generative models continue to accelerate on an eight-month doubling curve, the tension between sovereign regulatory philosophies and the inherently borderless nature of artificial intelligence remains the defining geopolitical and legal challenge of the decade.
