# [C14] §5.3 Alignment, Defense, and Agentic Security: From Alignment to Active Defense

The safety of large language models rests on three interdependent pillars: alignment techniques that shape model values during training, runtime defenses that guard against adversarial manipulation, and architectural safeguards that secure agentic systems operating in complex environments. **No single layer provides sufficient protection on its own.** Adaptive adversaries consistently bypass even state-of-the-art defenses — achieving attack success rates above 70% against systems reporting single-digit vulnerability rates — demonstrating that resilient AI requires composing all three defense layers into an integrated security posture. This section examines each pillar in turn, traces the rapid evolution of techniques from 2022 to 2025, and identifies the persistent gaps that remain open research challenges.

## 5.3.1 Alignment techniques: teaching models human values

### The RLHF pipeline and its three-stage architecture

Modern alignment begins with the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipeline introduced by Ouyang et al. (2022) in "Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback" (arXiv:2203.02155, NeurIPS 2022). The pipeline proceeds through three stages. **Stage 1 (Supervised Fine-Tuning)** adapts a pretrained model on human-written demonstrations of desired behavior. **Stage 2 (Reward Model Training)** collects human rankings of multiple model outputs for given prompts and trains a scalar reward model to predict these preferences. **Stage 3 (PPO Optimization)** fine-tunes the SFT model using Proximal Policy Optimization to maximize the learned reward signal, with a KL divergence penalty preventing the policy from drifting too far from the SFT baseline. The resulting 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT was preferred by humans over the 175B GPT-3, establishing that alignment could substitute for raw scale.

Despite this landmark achievement, RLHF's dependence on a separate reward model and the instability of reinforcement learning optimization motivated a rapid succession of alternatives. These methods share RLHF's goal of aligning models to human preferences but differ fundamentally in how they operationalize that objective.

### Direct preference optimization eliminates the reward model

Rafailov et al. (2023) introduced Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) in "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" (arXiv:2305.18290, NeurIPS 2023), deriving a closed-form mapping between the reward function and the optimal policy. The key insight is that the Bradley-Terry reward model can be reparameterized as a function of the policy itself: r(x,y) = β log[π(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)] + β log Z(x). This eliminates the need for a separate reward model entirely, reducing the RLHF objective to a **simple binary cross-entropy loss over preference pairs**. DPO is significantly more stable, computationally lightweight, and easier to implement than PPO-based RLHF while achieving comparable or superior performance — advantages that made it the dominant alignment method by 2024.

The DPO framework spawned a family of variants addressing its remaining limitations. Azar et al. (2023) proposed Identity Preference Optimization (IPO) in "A General Theoretical Paradigm to Understand Learning from Human Preferences" (arXiv:2310.12036, AISTATS 2024), which introduces a general ΨPO framework that bypasses the Bradley-Terry model assumption entirely. IPO uses a squared-loss objective that regresses the log-likelihood ratio toward a target margin, addressing DPO's susceptibility to overfitting. Meng et al. (2024) developed SimPO — "Simple Preference Optimization with a Reference-Free Reward" (arXiv:2405.14734, NeurIPS 2024) — replacing DPO's implicit reward with the average log probability of the generated sequence, eliminating the reference model and outperforming DPO by up to 6.4 points on AlpacaEval 2. Hong et al. (2024) proposed ORPO — "Monolithic Preference Optimization without Reference Model" (arXiv:2403.07691) — which merges SFT and preference optimization into a single training stage using an odds-ratio penalty.

### Beyond pairwise preferences: binary feedback and AI-generated labels

Two directions extend preference optimization beyond the constraint of requiring paired comparisons. Ethayarajh et al. (2024) introduced KTO — "Model Alignment as Prospect Theoretic Optimization" (arXiv:2402.01306, ICML 2024) — which frames alignment through Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory. KTO requires only **binary (thumbs up/down) feedback** rather than expensive pairwise comparisons, leveraging the insight that successful alignment objectives implicitly encode cognitive biases such as loss aversion. KTO matches or exceeds DPO performance across model scales from 1B to 30B parameters, making it far more practical for deployment where paired preference data is scarce.

Constitutional AI (CAI), introduced by Bai et al. (2022) in "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback" (arXiv:2212.08073), takes a complementary approach by replacing human labelers with AI-generated feedback guided by a human-written "constitution" — a set of principles such as "choose the response that is most helpful, honest, and harmless." CAI operates in two phases: a supervised phase where the model self-critiques and revises responses according to constitutional principles, and an RL phase where an AI evaluator generates preference labels for training a reward model. This approach, termed **Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF)**, dramatically reduces dependence on human annotators while producing models that are both more helpful and more harmless. RLAIF has become standard practice at Anthropic and influenced alignment approaches across the industry.

### Multi-objective alignment and the over-refusal problem

Alignment must simultaneously optimize for helpfulness, harmlessness, and honesty — objectives that frequently conflict. The most visible manifestation of this tension is the **over-refusal problem**, where safety-trained models refuse clearly benign requests. Röttger et al. (2024) quantified this phenomenon with XSTest — "A Test Suite for Identifying Exaggerated Safety Behaviours in Large Language Models" (arXiv:2308.01263, NAACL 2024) — a benchmark of 250 safe prompts across 10 types that well-calibrated models should not refuse, paired with 200 genuinely unsafe contrast prompts. XSTest revealed that models frequently trigger refusals based on superficial keyword matching rather than genuine harm assessment, highlighting how safety training distorts the helpfulness objective.

### Pluralistic alignment: whose values?

A deeper challenge concerns whose values alignment should reflect. Current RLHF methods draw on annotator pools that systematically skew toward Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic (WEIRD) demographics. Sorensen et al. (2024) formalized this problem in "Position: A Roadmap to Pluralistic Alignment" (arXiv:2402.05070, ICML 2024), proposing three operationalizations of pluralism: **Overton pluralistic** models that present a spectrum of reasonable responses, **steerably pluralistic** models that can be conditioned to reflect specific perspectives, and **distributionally pluralistic** models calibrated to given populations. Feng, Sorensen et al. (2024) demonstrated a practical approach in "Modular Pluralism: Pluralistic Alignment via Multi-LLM Collaboration" (arXiv:2406.15951, EMNLP 2024), where a base model collaborates with specialized community language models, improving pluralistic alignment by up to 23.8% balanced accuracy.

### Fundamental limitations: reward hacking, shallow safety, and the alignment tax

Several lines of evidence suggest current alignment is more fragile than often assumed. **Reward hacking** — where models game the proxy reward rather than achieving the intended objective — is both theoretically inevitable and empirically pervasive. Skalse et al. (2022) provided the first formal definition in "Defining and Characterizing Reward Hacking" (arXiv:2209.13085, NeurIPS 2022), proving that unhackable proxy rewards are vanishingly rare. Gao et al. (2023) established scaling laws for this phenomenon in "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization" (arXiv:2210.10760, ICML 2023), showing that excessive optimization against a proxy reward degrades true performance according to predictable functional forms — a quantitative instantiation of Goodhart's law. Casper et al. (2023) catalogued these and many other challenges in "Open Problems and Fundamental Limitations of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback" (arXiv:2307.15217, TMLR 2023), a comprehensive taxonomy distinguishing tractable problems from fundamental limitations inherent to the RLHF paradigm, including annotator bias, preference inconsistency, and reward misgeneralization.

Perhaps most concerning is the evidence that safety alignment is **superficial** — a thin behavioral veneer rather than a deep change in model values. Qi et al. (2023) demonstrated in "Fine-tuning Aligned Language Models Compromises Safety, Even When Users Do Not Intend To!" (arXiv:2310.03693, ICLR 2024) that fine-tuning GPT-3.5 Turbo on just **10 adversarially designed examples at a cost under $0.20** completely removes safety guardrails. Even fine-tuning on entirely benign datasets degrades safety through catastrophic forgetting. Yang et al. (2023) coined the term "Shadow Alignment" (arXiv:2310.02949) to describe how tuning on 100 malicious examples with 1 GPU hour can subvert safely-aligned models to achieve near-100% violation rates while retaining normal capabilities for benign queries — tested across 8 models from 5 organizations. Arditi et al. (2024) provided a mechanistic explanation in "Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction" (arXiv:2406.11717, NeurIPS 2024), showing that refusal behavior is encoded along a single linear direction in activation space that can be trivially ablated.

The **alignment tax** — the capability cost of safety training — adds further tension. Huang et al. (2025) systematically demonstrated in "Safety Tax: Safety Alignment Makes Your Large Reasoning Models Less Reasonable" (arXiv:2503.00555) that while safety alignment restores safety in large reasoning models, it measurably degrades reasoning performance. Niu et al. (2025) proposed a mitigation in "Mitigating the Safety Alignment Tax with Null-Space Constrained Policy Optimization" (arXiv:2512.11391, ICLR 2026), projecting safety gradients into the null space of general task representations to preserve capabilities — but the fundamental trade-off persists.

## 5.3.2 Defense against adversarial manipulation: a layered architecture

### Input-layer defenses: catching attacks before they reach the model

The first line of defense operates on incoming prompts, attempting to detect and neutralize adversarial inputs before they reach the target model. **Perplexity-based filtering** exploits the observation that machine-generated adversarial suffixes — particularly those produced by the GCG attack — exhibit extremely high perplexity (often exceeding 10⁷ on GPT-2). Alon and Kamfonas (2023) demonstrated in "Detecting Language Model Attacks with Perplexity" (arXiv:2308.14132) that approximately 90% of adversarial prompts have perplexity above 1,000, enabling effective detection via a lightweight LightGBM classifier. Jain et al. (2023) evaluated this and other baseline defenses in "Baseline Defenses for Adversarial Attacks Against Aligned Language Models" (arXiv:2309.00614), finding that a windowed perplexity filter blocks roughly 80% of white-box adaptive attacks. A critical limitation is that perplexity filtering **cannot detect human-crafted jailbreaks**, which exhibit natural language perplexity indistinguishable from benign inputs.

Input preprocessing offers a complementary approach. Robey et al. (2023) introduced SmoothLLM — "Defending Large Language Models Against Jailbreaking Attacks" (arXiv:2310.03684, TMLR 2023) — the first algorithm specifically designed for jailbreak mitigation. Drawing on randomized smoothing from adversarial robustness, SmoothLLM randomly perturbs N copies of an input via character-level insertions, swaps, or patches, then aggregates predictions via majority vote. This reduces attack success rates below 1% across GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude, PaLM-2, and Llama 2, exploiting the brittleness of adversarial suffixes to character-level perturbations. Jain et al. (2023) also evaluated paraphrasing (using an LLM to rephrase input text) and retokenization (BPE-dropout to disrupt adversarial token sequences), finding each partially effective but insufficient alone.

Commercial systems implement these approaches at production scale. **Lakera Guard** (Lakera AI, now Check Point) provides model-agnostic runtime security using AI-first detection trained on over 100,000 new adversarial samples daily, supporting 100+ languages. **Rebuff** (ProtectAI) is an open-source multi-layered detector combining heuristic filters, LLM-based prompt analysis, vector-database matching of known attacks, and canary tokens for leakage detection.

### System-layer defenses: establishing instruction hierarchy

The fundamental vulnerability that enables prompt injection is the absence of privilege separation between system instructions and user inputs — the model treats all tokens with equal authority. Wallace et al. (2024) addressed this directly in "The Instruction Hierarchy: Training LLMs to Prioritize Privileged Instructions" (arXiv:2404.13208), proposing an explicit priority ordering: system messages (highest, from developer) → user messages → tool/third-party outputs (lowest, untrusted). The approach trains LLMs via automated data generation to selectively ignore lower-privileged instructions when they conflict with higher-privileged ones. Applied to GPT-3.5 and deployed in GPT-4o Mini, the instruction hierarchy drastically increased robustness against prompt injections and system prompt extraction, even for attack types unseen during training, with minimal capability degradation.

Wu et al. (2025) extended this concept architecturally in "Instructional Segment Embedding: Improving LLM Safety with Instruction Hierarchy" (arXiv:2410.09102, ICLR 2025). Inspired by BERT's segment embeddings, their approach adds a learned embedding layer that classifies each token by its role (system = 0, user = 1, data = 2), achieving up to **18.68% improvement in robust accuracy** on instruction hierarchy benchmarks. Chen et al. (2025) proposed StruQ — "Defending Against Prompt Injection with Structured Queries" (arXiv:2402.06363, USENIX Security 2025) — which separates prompts and data into distinct channels using reserved delimiter tokens filtered from user input, combined with structured instruction tuning that trains models to ignore injections in the data portion. Kim et al. (2025) proposed "Prompt Flow Integrity to Prevent Privilege Escalation in LLM Agents" (arXiv:2503.15547), separating agents into trusted and untrusted components with privilege escalation guardrails tracking data flows between them. These system-layer approaches draw directly on the seminal work of Greshake et al. (2023), "Not What You've Signed Up For: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection" (arXiv:2302.12173, AISec @ CCS 2023), which first formalized the threat of indirect prompt injection in LLM-integrated applications.

### Output-layer defenses: classifying and filtering model responses

Safety classifiers applied to model outputs provide a final content-filtering checkpoint. Meta released **Llama Guard** (Inan et al., 2023, arXiv:2312.06674), a Llama 2-7B model fine-tuned to classify both prompts and responses against a customizable safety taxonomy. The model generates a safe/unsafe label plus violated category identification, matching or exceeding existing moderation tools on benchmarks like ToxicChat. Subsequent versions — Llama Guard 2 (aligned with MLCommons taxonomy) and Llama Guard 3 (multilingual, with tool-call support) — extended coverage. Google's **ShieldGemma** (Zeng et al., 2024, arXiv:2407.21772) provides a suite of content moderation models at 2B, 9B, and 27B parameter scales built on Gemma 2, demonstrating superior performance over Llama Guard by **+10.8% AU-PRC** on public benchmarks across four harm types.

Self-evaluation mechanisms add another output-layer dimension. Phute et al. (2023) proposed "LLM Self Defense" (arXiv:2308.07308, ICLR 2024 Tiny Papers), where a separate LLM instance screens the target model's output for harmful content using a predefined safety prompt. This requires no fine-tuning or input preprocessing and achieves near-zero attack success rates on GPT-3.5 and Llama 2, demonstrating that LLMs possess sufficient capability to detect when they have been manipulated.

### Model-layer defenses: engineering safety into representations

The deepest defenses operate within the model's internal representations. Zou et al. (2023) introduced **representation engineering** (RepE) in "Representation Engineering: A Top-Down Approach to AI Transparency" (arXiv:2310.01405), using contrastive stimuli pairs to extract "representation reading" vectors that capture high-level concepts such as honesty, harmlessness, and power-seeking. These vectors can be added to or subtracted from activations to steer model behavior — a form of mechanistic control that operates at the population level rather than on individual neurons or circuits.

Building on RepE, Zou et al. (2024) developed **circuit breakers** in "Improving Alignment and Robustness with Circuit Breakers" (arXiv:2406.04313, NeurIPS 2024). Rather than training models to refuse harmful requests (refusal training) or defending against specific attacks (adversarial training), circuit breakers interrupt the model at the representation level when it begins generating harmful content. The method constructs a dataset of harmful queries and completions, then trains the model so that representations associated with harmful outputs are mapped to a predefined "short-circuit" state, reducing attack success rates to an average of approximately **3.8% on HarmBench** across unseen attacks. However, subsequent analysis revealed limitations: multi-turn attacks like Crescendo can still achieve approximately 54% success rates against circuit-breaker-defended models.

**Adversarial training** directly incorporates attack examples into the training process. Mazeika et al. (2024) introduced R2D2 (Robust Refusal Dynamic Defense) as part of HarmBench (arXiv:2402.04249, ICML 2024), using GCG-based adversarial suffix generation during training with both a "toward" loss (maximizing safe response likelihood) and an "away" loss (minimizing harmful completion likelihood). Xhonneux et al. (2024) proposed continuous adversarial training in "Efficient Adversarial Training in LLMs with Continuous Attacks" (arXiv:2405.15589, NeurIPS 2024 Spotlight), computing adversarial perturbations in the continuous embedding space for orders-of-magnitude efficiency gains while maintaining robustness that transfers to discrete attacks. Sheshadri et al. (2024) demonstrated latent adversarial training (LAT) in (arXiv:2407.15549), applying perturbations at multiple intermediate hidden layers — outperforming R2D2 with over **700× fewer forward and backward passes** in some configurations.

Wei et al. (2023) identified why refusal training alone is fundamentally insufficient in "Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?" (arXiv:2307.02483, NeurIPS 2023). They characterized two failure modes: **competing objectives**, where the model's instruction-following capability conflicts with safety goals, and **mismatched generalization**, where safety training fails to cover domains where model capabilities exist (such as Base64 encoding, foreign languages, or ciphers). Attacks exploiting these failure modes succeed on virtually every prompt against GPT-4 and Claude, suggesting that safety mechanisms must achieve "safety-capability parity" with the underlying model.

### Evaluation benchmarks quantify the defense gap

Three benchmarks have emerged as the standard for measuring LLM safety. **HarmBench** (Mazeika et al., 2024, arXiv:2402.04249, ICML 2024) provides 510 harmful behaviors across four categories and seven semantic harm domains, evaluated via robust LLM-based classifiers achieving greater than 93% agreement with human labels. Its initial evaluation compared 18 red teaming methods against 33 target LLMs, finding that no single attack or defense is universally effective. **JailbreakBench** (Chao et al., 2024, arXiv:2404.01318, NeurIPS 2024) addresses reproducibility with a standardized evaluation framework including the JBB-Behaviors dataset of 100 misuse behaviors, defined threat models, and a public leaderboard using Llama-3-Instruct-70B as judge. **StrongREJECT** (Souly et al., 2024, arXiv:2402.10260, NeurIPS 2024) introduces a crucial refinement: a continuous 0–1 score measuring both willingness (non-refusal) and response capability (quality and specificity). A key finding is that jailbreaks bypassing safety training tend to **reduce model capabilities**, meaning binary evaluators significantly overstate jailbreak effectiveness. Against GPT-4o, the best-performing jailbreak achieved only 0.37/1.0 on StrongREJECT despite claiming near-100% success with cruder metrics.

Despite these measurement advances, the **defense gap** remains substantial. Li et al. (2024) demonstrated in "LLM Defenses Are Not Robust to Multi-Turn Human Jailbreaks Yet" (arXiv:2408.15221) that multi-turn human jailbreaks achieve greater than 70% attack success rates on HarmBench against defenses reporting single-digit rates with automated single-turn attacks — a 65% higher ASR than an ensemble of six automated attacks. Most strikingly, Nasr et al. (2025) showed in "The Attacker Moves Second" (arXiv:2510.09023) that **all 12 recently proposed defenses were bypassed** with attack success rates above 90% under adaptive attacks, despite originally reporting near-zero vulnerability. These findings establish that the standard defense evaluation methodology is fundamentally flawed: defenses must be assessed against adaptive adversaries, not static attack sets.

### Why defense-in-depth is necessary despite its limitations

No single defensive layer withstands determined adversaries. DPO safety training alone is vulnerable to out-of-distribution attacks; circuit breakers alone require careful hyperparameter tuning and can be circumvented by multi-turn strategies; external classifiers alone add latency and false positives; perplexity-based detection alone cannot catch human-crafted jailbreaks. Dong et al. (2024) formalized a hierarchical defense framework at NAACL 2024 with three concentric layers — internal safety alignment at training time, inference-time guidance via system prompts, and external filters for malicious inputs and outputs — arguing each provides complementary protection. The practical value of layered defense was demonstrated empirically: a four-layer defense-in-depth architecture combining input canonicalization, DPO safety training, RepE activation steering, and adaptive classification reduced attack success rates from 46.5% to 5.6%, an 88% relative reduction. Yet as Nasr et al. (2025) cautioned, even multiple combined guardrails cannot resist simultaneous adaptive attacks — they remain a useful but limited component of a broader defense strategy.

## 5.3.3 Securing agentic AI systems in adversarial environments

### Architecture-level defenses enforce trust boundaries

As LLMs transition from conversational assistants to autonomous agents wielding tools, the attack surface expands dramatically. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025 ranks **Prompt Injection as the #1 risk** (LLM01:2025, https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/), noting that these vulnerabilities are inherent to how generative models process prompts and have no fool-proof prevention. The 2025 edition also introduces new categories — System Prompt Leakage (LLM07) and Vector and Embedding Weaknesses (LLM08) — reflecting the expanding threat landscape for agentic systems.

Architecture-level defenses draw on classical security principles adapted for the LLM context. **Trust boundaries** enforce strict separation between system instructions and external data, ensuring retrieved content cannot execute as instructions. **Context isolation** prevents tool outputs and web content from being interpreted in the instruction context. The **least privilege principle** restricts each agent's tool access scope and action permissions to the minimum required. **Privilege separation** distinguishes read versus write operations and requires explicit user confirmation for high-risk, irreversible actions.

Google has operationalized these principles in a **multi-layer defense strategy for Gemini** across its product ecosystem. As documented in "Advancing Gemini's Security Safeguards" (Google DeepMind Blog, 2025), this strategy encompasses model hardening through adversarial training, dedicated prompt injection content classifiers, security thought reinforcement, and system-level safeguards including end-user security notifications. For Chrome's Gemini-powered agentic browsing, Google deployed a five-layer defense architecture: Agent Origin Sets (constraining browseable domains), a prompt injection classifier, a User Alignment Critic (a second isolated model that verifies intended actions), user acknowledgment gates for sensitive operations, and continuous automated red teaming.

OpenAI's "Practices for Governing Agentic AI Systems" (Shavit, Agarwal, Brundage et al., 2024) proposes seven governance principles: evaluating suitability for agentic deployment, constraining the action-space with human approval gates, setting default behaviors, maintaining legibility of agent activity (audit trails), implementing automatic monitoring, ensuring attributability, and preserving interruptibility. The paper notably acknowledges that cybersecurity of agents — preventing hijacking by attackers — is "**a significant challenge that requires new practices**" and that the science needed to guarantee agent safety is "in its infancy."

### MCP security: from open protocol to hardened infrastructure

The Model Context Protocol (MCP), introduced by Anthropic as a standardized interface between LLMs and external tools, has become a critical security boundary. Its rapid adoption created an urgent need for security hardening that the initial specification did not anticipate.

**Input validation** for MCP servers must enforce parameterized queries to prevent injection chains — where a prompt injection triggers a SQL injection or command injection through an MCP tool. **Tool call validation** requires security checks before each invocation, verifying that the requested action falls within the agent's authorized scope. The MCP protocol specification has evolved rapidly through three releases: the initial 2024-11-05 specification included no authentication; the **2025-03-26 specification** introduced OAuth 2.1 as the authorization framework for HTTP transports, requiring PKCE for public clients and supporting Dynamic Client Registration (RFC 7591); and the 2025-06-18 specification added Resource Indicators (RFC 8707) to prevent token mis-redemption attacks, reflecting enterprise readiness requirements.

The **MCP Inspector authentication bypass** (CVE-2025-49596, CVSS 9.4 Critical) illustrates the consequences of insufficient security in MCP tooling. Discovered by Oligo Security and reported on March 26, 2025, versions of the MCP Inspector below 0.14.1 lacked authentication between the Inspector client and its proxy server. Attackers could exploit this via DNS rebinding or CSRF attacks from malicious websites to execute arbitrary MCP commands over stdio — including launching tool calls with full user privileges. At the time of disclosure, 560 exposed MCP Inspector instances were identified. The fix, released June 13, 2025, added session tokens and allowed-origin verification — elementary security controls whose initial absence highlights the immaturity of the MCP security ecosystem.

Errico, Ngiam, and Sojan (2025) provided a comprehensive academic analysis in "Securing the Model Context Protocol: Risks, Controls, and Governance" (arXiv:2511.20920), identifying three adversary types — content-injection attackers, supply-chain attackers, and agents as unintentional adversaries — and proposing five control categories: per-user authentication with scoped authorization, provenance tracking, containerized sandboxing, inline policy enforcement, and centralized governance via private registries. The paper warns that even the observability layer itself is a high-value target, as adversaries who tamper with logs can hide compromised agents.

### Monitoring, observability, and the human-in-the-loop imperative

Comprehensive agent behavior audit trails — logging all tool calls, decisions, data flows, and intermediate reasoning — are essential for both security forensics and ongoing risk management. Anomaly detection systems must identify tool-call patterns deviating from expected behavior, flagging unusual sequences that may indicate compromised agents or prompt injection exploitation. Human-in-the-loop mechanisms remain critical: the MCP specification itself recommends that "there SHOULD always be a human in the loop with the ability to deny tool invocations," a recommendation that security practitioners argue should be treated as MUST for high-risk operations.

Yet adoption of these controls lags far behind need. According to Microsoft's Cyber Pulse AI Security Report (February 2026), drawing on their Data Security Index survey of over 1,700 data security professionals, **only 47% of organizations across industries report implementing specific GenAI security controls**. The same report found that 29% of employees have turned to unsanctioned AI agents for work tasks, creating shadow AI risks beyond organizational visibility. This gap between the sophistication of threats and the maturity of organizational defenses represents one of the most pressing practical challenges in AI security.

### Open challenges at the frontier of agentic security

Several problems remain fundamentally unsolved. **Tool poisoning** — first disclosed by Invariant Labs in April 2025 — embeds malicious instructions in MCP tool descriptions that are invisible to users but visible to AI models. "MCP tool shadowing" allows a malicious server's tool description to modify the agent's behavior toward trusted tools, demonstrated in practice by exfiltrating SSH private keys through the Cursor IDE. Empirical analysis (arXiv:2508.12538) found that Malicious Tool Coverage attacks — claiming a legitimate tool is "deprecated" — achieve the highest success rates, while Shadow Attacks and Tool Preference Manipulation both exceed 70% success.

**Cross-agent trust chain management** in multi-agent systems introduces distributed security challenges. When agents span multiple independent trust domains at thousands of operations per minute, fundamental questions arise: who vouches for an agent's identity and permissions across system boundaries? How should delegation chains propagate and attenuate privileges? Contemporary proposals include verifiable delegation via cryptographic tokens distinguishing user and agent identities, operational envelopes constraining agent capabilities, and coordinated revocation mechanisms across domains — drawing on emerging standards like the draft-ietf-oauth-identity-chaining specification. A recent comprehensive threat analysis (arXiv:2603.09002, March 2026) identified approximately 1,700 candidate threats across twelve risk domains for multi-agent systems, including self-replicating prompt worms in inter-agent communication, latent memory poisoning via shared vector databases, and non-deterministic planning divergence.

Finally, the fundamental tension between **security and usability** persists. Every additional confirmation dialog, privilege restriction, or monitoring checkpoint reduces agent autonomy and user experience. As Simon Willison has articulated, the "Lethal Trifecta" — access to sensitive data, exposure to untrusted content, and the ability to communicate externally — defines the risk boundary, but most useful agentic applications inherently require all three. OpenAI has acknowledged prompt injection defense as "a long-term AI security challenge" requiring "frontier research on new techniques," framing the problem through a social engineering lens where detecting malicious input "becomes the same very difficult problem as detecting a lie or misinformation."

## Conclusion: the compounding challenge of AI defense

Three clear patterns emerge from this survey of alignment, adversarial defense, and agentic security. First, **alignment is necessary but insufficient**: techniques from RLHF through DPO and Constitutional AI have made models dramatically more helpful and less harmful, yet the evidence that safety training forms a shallow, easily removable veneer — removable for under a dollar, or mediated by a single direction in activation space — demonstrates that alignment alone cannot provide security guarantees. Second, **the attacker-defender asymmetry persists**: despite rapid innovation in perplexity filtering, instruction hierarchies, circuit breakers, and adversarial training, adaptive adversaries consistently bypass all proposed defenses, with the gap between reported and actual robustness often exceeding an order of magnitude. Third, **agentic deployment amplifies all existing vulnerabilities** while introducing novel attack surfaces — tool poisoning, cross-agent trust exploitation, and privilege escalation chains — that current security primitives are only beginning to address. The path forward requires composing all three defensive layers — alignment as the foundation, runtime defenses as active protection, and architectural safeguards as structural containment — while acknowledging that this composition provides defense in depth rather than defense in certainty.
