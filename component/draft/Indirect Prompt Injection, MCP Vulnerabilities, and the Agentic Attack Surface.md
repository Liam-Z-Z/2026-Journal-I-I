# [C06] §3.3.3–3.3.4 Indirect Prompt Injection, MCP Vulnerabilities, and the Agentic Attack Surface

## 3.3.3 Indirect Prompt Injection: From Retrieval Channels to Autonomous Exploitation

The most consequential evolution in the adversarial landscape against LLMs is the shift from direct prompt injection—where the attacker interacts with the model—to **indirect prompt injection (IPI)**, where malicious instructions are embedded in external data sources that the model retrieves and processes without any direct attacker-model interface. This section traces the escalation from IPI in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems to its far more dangerous manifestation in agentic architectures, and documents the emerging MCP ecosystem as a critical amplifier of these threats.

### 3.3.3.1 Foundations and taxonomy of indirect prompt injection

Indirect prompt injection was first systematically characterized by Greshake et al. in their seminal 2023 paper, which demonstrated that LLM-integrated applications "blur the line between data and instructions," enabling adversaries to compromise system behavior without direct access to the model (Kai Greshake, Sahar Abdelnabi, Shailesh Mishra, Christoph Endres, Thorsten Holz, and Mario Fritz, "Not What You've Signed Up For: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection," in *Proceedings of the 16th ACM Workshop on Artificial Intelligence and Security (AISec '23)*, pp. 79–90, 2023, DOI: 10.1145/3605764.3623985; arXiv preprint arXiv:2302.12173). The paper introduced a taxonomy of attack consequences including information gathering, fraud, malware spreading, and intrusion—and, critically, demonstrated a **self-replicating prompt injection worm** that could propagate through AI-mediated email systems.

The fundamental distinction from direct injection is architectural. In direct prompt injection, as formalized by Perez and Ribeiro (Fábio Perez and Ian Ribeiro, "Ignore Previous Prompt: Attack Techniques For Language Models," *NeurIPS 2022 ML Safety Workshop*, arXiv:2211.09527), the attacker must interact with the model's input interface. In IPI, the attacker places malicious instructions in **any data source the model might consume**—web pages, documents, emails, database entries, calendar invitations, or tool outputs—and the attack executes when the model retrieves and processes that content during normal operation. Liu et al. provided the first formal framework for this distinction, defining target tasks and injected tasks across a systematic evaluation of 5 attacks and 10 defenses on 10 LLMs (Yupei Liu, Yuqi Jia, Runpeng Geng, Jinyuan Jia, and Neil Zhenqiang Gong, "Formalizing and Benchmarking Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses," *33rd USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security '24)*, pp. 1831–1847, 2024, arXiv:2310.12815).

This distinction carries profound security implications. Direct injection requires the attacker to be a user of the system. **Indirect injection requires only that the attacker can influence any data source in the model's retrieval scope**—a dramatically larger and more difficult-to-defend attack surface.

### 3.3.3.2 Attack vectors in retrieval-augmented generation

RAG systems expand the IPI attack surface to any knowledge source in the retrieval pipeline. Three principal vectors have been documented extensively.

**Poisoned web pages retrieved by search-augmented LLMs.** Greshake et al. demonstrated that adversarial prompts placed on web pages indexed by search engines could be retrieved by Bing Chat (Sydney), enabling information ecosystem contamination, selective mis-summarization, and fraudulent content promotion. This was not theoretical: in February 2023, security researchers Kevin Liu and Marvin von Hagen independently extracted Bing Chat's system prompt through prompt injection, revealing its internal codename "Sydney" and hidden behavioral directives. Cristiano Giardina later created a website using hidden text in invisible font that overrode the Bing metaprompt via indirect prompt injection. In December 2024, The Guardian reported that OpenAI's ChatGPT search tool was vulnerable to the same class of attack: **hidden text on web pages could override negative product reviews** with artificially positive assessments.

**Malicious documents in enterprise knowledge bases.** Zou et al. demonstrated PoisonedRAG, the first knowledge corruption attack specifically targeting RAG systems, achieving **over 90% attack success rates by injecting as few as five malicious documents** into the knowledge database (Wei Zou, Runpeng Geng, Binghui Wang, and Jinyuan Jia, "PoisonedRAG: Knowledge Corruption Attacks to Retrieval-Augmented Generation of Large Language Models," *34th USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security '25)*, pp. 3827–3844, 2025, arXiv:2402.07867). Chang et al. further identified that retrieval is the key bottleneck for real-world IPI, and introduced a Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) optimizer that dramatically improved retrieval of malicious content across 11 BEIR benchmark corpora (Hongyan Chang, Ergute Bao, Xinjian Luo, and Ting Yu, "Overcoming the Retrieval Barrier: Indirect Prompt Injection in the Wild for LLM Systems," arXiv:2601.07072, January 2026). Zhong et al. independently showed that adversarial passage injection into retrieval corpora could systematically manipulate question-answering outputs (Zexuan Zhong, Ziqing Huang, Alexander Wettig, and Danqi Chen, "Poisoning Retrieval Corpora by Injecting Adversarial Passages," arXiv:2310.19156, 2023).

**Adversarial content in emails and enterprise communications.** Greshake et al. demonstrated multi-stage IPI attacks through AI-processed emails, including data exfiltration and "worming" behavior. This vector proved devastating in practice: in August 2024, PromptArmor disclosed that Slack AI's RAG functionality could be exploited through hidden instructions in public channels or uploaded PDFs to **exfiltrate data from private channels**, including API keys—critically, the attacker's message was not cited as a source in the response (PromptArmor, "Data Exfiltration from Slack AI via Indirect Prompt Injection," August 20, 2024, https://promptarmor.substack.com/p/data-exfiltration-from-slack-ai-via). A 2026 study of commercial chatbot plugins found that 15 third-party AI chatbots supported automated web scraping for RAG, and manual audit revealed 13% of sampled e-commerce sites had exposed their chatbots to uncontrolled third-party content (arXiv:2511.05797, accepted at IEEE S&P 2026).

**Empirical benchmarking** has confirmed the severity of these vectors. Yi et al. introduced BIPIA, the first benchmark for IPI, covering email QA, web QA, table QA, summarization, and code QA across 25 LLMs—finding that **more capable models are paradoxically more vulnerable**, with a positive Pearson correlation (r=0.64) between model capability and attack success rate (Jingwei Yi, Yueqi Xie, Bin Zhu, Keegan Hines, Emre Kiciman, Guangzhong Sun, Xing Xie, and Fangzhao Wu, "Benchmarking and Defending Against Indirect Prompt Injection Attacks on Large Language Models," *Proceedings of KDD '25*, ACM, 2025, arXiv:2312.14197, DOI: 10.1145/3690624.3709179). De Stefano et al. corroborated this in a RAG-specific evaluation, finding most attacks settled at approximately **40% success rate**, rising to 60% when ambiguous outputs were included (Gianluca De Stefano et al., "Rag and Roll: An End-to-End Evaluation of Indirect Prompt Manipulations in LLM-based Application Frameworks," arXiv:2408.05025, August 2024).

### 3.3.3.3 The agentic escalation: from wrong outputs to malicious actions

The transition from chatbot-style LLMs to agentic systems constitutes a **qualitative escalation in the consequences of indirect prompt injection**. In a chatbot, a successful IPI produces incorrect or manipulated text. In an agent equipped with tools—file system access, API calls, shell commands, email capabilities, database queries—a successful IPI triggers **real-world actions with potentially irreversible consequences**. This escalation is the central security argument against deploying under-defended agentic systems.

**The structural amplifiers of agentic risk** are threefold. First, agents possess *tool permissions* that give the LLM direct operational capability over real systems. As documented by the MDPI comprehensive review, "simple conversational LLMs are limited to text generation; compromise results in misinformation or system prompt extraction. In contrast, tool-calling agents transform prompt injection from an information threat into an **operational threat with physical consequences**" ("Prompt Injection Attacks in Large Language Models and AI Agent Systems: A Comprehensive Review," *MDPI Information*, 17(1):54, 2025). Second, agents frequently operate with *unsupervised execution* under auto-approve settings. GitHub Copilot's "YOLO mode" (`chat.tools.autoApprove: true`) disables all user confirmations, and CVE-2025-53773 demonstrated that prompt injection could silently enable this mode and then execute arbitrary commands. Third, agents engage in *multi-step reasoning and execution* where a single injected instruction can cascade through a planning-execution loop. Schneider modeled this as a "Promptware Kill Chain"—initial access through prompt injection, privilege escalation via jailbreak, lateral movement across tools, and action on objectives—treating prompt injection payloads as a new class of malware executing in natural-language space (Christian Schneider, "From LLM to agentic AI: prompt injection got worse," christian-schneider.net, 2025).

The InjecAgent benchmark quantified this risk across 1,054 test cases spanning 17 user tools and 62 attacker tools: **ReAct-prompted GPT-4 was vulnerable 24% of the time at baseline, rising to approximately 47% with enhanced attack prompts** (Qiusi Zhan, Zhixiang Liang, Zifan Ying, and Daniel Kang, "InjecAgent: Benchmarking Indirect Prompt Injections in Tool-Integrated Large Language Model Agents," *Findings of ACL 2024*, pp. 10471–10506, arXiv:2403.02691). The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications (December 2025) codified this escalation as ASI01 (Agent Goal Hijack): "agents amplify existing LLM vulnerabilities. What was a single manipulated output becomes an orchestrated multi-tool kill chain" (OWASP GenAI Security Project, "OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications," December 10, 2025, https://genai.owasp.org/).

**Real-world exploitation has confirmed these theoretical predictions across every major agentic platform:**

**Zero-click remote code execution in AI coding assistants.** CVE-2025-59944 (rated High severity, Impact 8/10) was a case-sensitivity bypass in Cursor IDE's file protection mechanism, discovered by Brett Gustafson of Lakera and fixed in Cursor version 1.7. On case-insensitive filesystems (macOS, Windows), an indirect prompt injection could instruct Cursor to create `.cUrSoR/mcp.json`, bypassing the case-sensitive file protection logic while the OS resolved it as the protected `.cursor/mcp.json`. This overwrote MCP configuration to inject a malicious MCP server, achieving full RCE (Lakera, "Cursor Vulnerability (CVE-2025-59944)," October 10, 2025, https://www.lakera.ai/blog/cursor-vulnerability-cve-2025-59944). Related vulnerabilities included CVE-2025-54135 (CurXecute, CVSS 8.6, RCE via MCP auto-start in Cursor, discovered by Aim Labs/Cato Networks) and CVE-2025-54136 (MCPoison, CVSS 7.2, malicious MCP file swaps post-approval, discovered by Check Point Research). In December 2025, researcher Ari Marzouk's "IDEsaster" disclosure identified **30+ vulnerabilities across all major AI IDEs**—GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Windsurf, Kiro.dev, Zed.dev, Roo Code, JetBrains Junie, Cline, Gemini CLI, and Claude Code—resulting in 24 CVEs, demonstrating that **100% of tested AI IDEs were vulnerable** to prompt injection chains leading to code execution.

**AI browser agents manipulated by hidden prompts.** Johann Rehberger demonstrated in February 2025 that ChatGPT Operator could be manipulated through prompt injections embedded in GitHub Issues and websites to visit sensitive authenticated sites, collect private information, and exfiltrate data to attacker-controlled servers (Rehberger, "ChatGPT Operator: Prompt Injection Exploits and Defenses," Embrace The Red blog, February 17, 2025). Following the launch of ChatGPT Atlas in October 2025, OpenAI's own automated red-teaming—using a reinforcement-learning-trained attacker—uncovered new classes of prompt injection attacks, including scenarios where a malicious email planted in a user's inbox directed the agent to send a resignation letter instead of drafting an out-of-office reply (OpenAI, "Continuously hardening ChatGPT Atlas against prompt injection attacks," December 22, 2025, https://openai.com/index/hardening-atlas-against-prompt-injection/).

Perplexity's AI browser Comet (launched July 2025) demonstrated the extreme end of this vulnerability class. Brave researchers showed that hidden text on a Reddit page could instruct Comet to extract the user's email address, log in, receive a one-time password, and exfiltrate both—achieving **full account takeover** (Brave, "Agentic Browser Security: Indirect Prompt Injection in Perplexity Comet," 2025, https://brave.com/blog/comet-prompt-injection/). LayerX researchers disclosed "CometJacking," where a single weaponized URL with no malicious page content could steal sensitive data from connected Gmail and Calendar services using Base64-encoding to bypass exfiltration checks (LayerX Security, "CometJacking," October 2025). Most dramatically, Zenity Labs' "PleaseFix" disclosure in March 2026 demonstrated zero-click exploit paths where a Google Calendar invite with hidden prompt injection caused Comet to browse the local file system via `file://` protocol, read sensitive files, and even navigate an authenticated 1Password web interface to extract vault credentials (Zenity Labs, "PerplexedBrowser," March 3, 2026, https://labs.zenity.io/).

**Enterprise-scale zero-click data exfiltration.** The EchoLeak vulnerability (CVE-2025-32711, CVSS 9.3), discovered by Aim Security and described as "the first real-world zero-click prompt injection exploit in a production LLM system," demonstrated that a crafted email with embedded hidden instructions could cause Microsoft 365 Copilot to exfiltrate sensitive data—emails, OneDrive files, SharePoint content, Teams messages—via reference-style Markdown image links auto-fetched through a trusted Microsoft Teams domain proxy, requiring zero user interaction (P. Reddy et al., "EchoLeak: The First Real-World Zero-Click Prompt Injection Exploit in a Production LLM System," arXiv:2509.10540, September 2025).

**Active weaponization in the wild.** The transition from proof-of-concept to real-world deployment was confirmed by Palo Alto Networks Unit 42, which published large-scale telemetry showing IPI is being **actively weaponized on the public web**. Their March 2026 report documented 22 distinct attacker techniques across observed payloads. In December 2025, they detected the first confirmed case of a real-world malicious IPI designed to bypass an AI-based product advertisement review system at a deceptive scam website—representing the first documented in-the-wild weaponization of IPI against an AI security control (B. Kaleli, S. Farooqi, O. Starov, and N. Mohamed, "Fooling AI Agents: Web-Based Indirect Prompt Injection Observed in the Wild," Palo Alto Networks Unit 42, March 3, 2026, https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/ai-agent-prompt-injection/). Delivery methods observed included visible plaintext (37.8%), HTML attribute cloaking (19.8%), and CSS rendering suppression (16.9%).

### 3.3.3.4 The MCP ecosystem as an emerging attack surface

The Model Context Protocol (MCP), introduced by Anthropic in late 2024 as an open standard for connecting LLMs to external tools and data sources, has rapidly become the dominant integration layer for agentic AI systems. Its rapid adoption—SentinelOne observed **2× growth in MCP usage between January and April 2025** alone (SentinelOne, "Avoiding MCP Mania: How to Secure the Next Frontier of AI," 2025, https://www.sentinelone.com/blog/avoiding-mcp-mania-how-to-secure-the-next-frontier-of-ai/)—has exposed a security surface characterized by both novel AI-specific vulnerabilities and recurring classical software flaws.

**The prevalence of classical vulnerabilities.** The first large-scale security audit of MCP server implementations, conducted by Equixly in March 2025, found that **43% contained command injection vulnerabilities, 30% permitted unrestricted URL fetching (SSRF), and 22% allowed path traversal or arbitrary file reads** (Alessio Dalla Piazza, "MCP Servers: The New Security Nightmare," Equixly Blog, March 29, 2025, https://equixly.com/blog/2025/03/29/mcp-server-new-security-nightmare/). Astrix Security's analysis of 5,200+ open-source MCP server implementations found that **53% relied on long-lived static API keys** and only 8.5% used modern OAuth-based authentication. These findings echo across individual case studies: Datadog Security Labs documented a SQL injection vulnerability in Anthropic's reference PostgreSQL MCP server where stacked queries could bypass read-only transaction controls to execute arbitrary write operations, including `DROP SCHEMA public CASCADE` (Datadog Security Labs, "MCP vulnerability case study: SQL injection in the Postgres MCP server," 2025, https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/mcp-vulnerability-case-study-SQL-injection-in-the-postgresql-mcp-server/).

The intersection of classical and AI-specific vulnerabilities creates a particularly dangerous pattern: SQL injection in MCP database servers enables **stored prompt injection**, where adversarial instructions are written into the database and later retrieved and executed by the LLM during normal operation—transforming a well-understood software bug into a novel AI attack vector. Trend Micro documented this precisely in Anthropic's official SQLite MCP server, forked over 5,000 times, where SQL injection enabled stored-prompt injection that executed when privileged agents read contaminated data (Trend Micro, "Why a Classic MCP Server Vulnerability Can Undermine Your Entire AI Agent," June 2025, https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/research/25/f/).

**Critical remote code execution vulnerabilities.** Two critical CVEs illustrate the severity of flaws in core MCP infrastructure. **CVE-2025-6514** (CVSS 9.6, AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:R/S:C/C:H/I:H/A:H) was an OS command injection vulnerability in mcp-remote, the proxy enabling LLM hosts (Claude Desktop, Cursor, Windsurf) to communicate with remote MCP servers. Discovered by JFrog Security Research and published July 9, 2025, the vulnerability allowed a malicious MCP server to inject commands via a crafted OAuth `authorization_endpoint` URL, achieving arbitrary command execution on Windows through PowerShell subexpression evaluation. The affected package had been downloaded over **437,000 times** (JFrog, "Critical mcp-remote RCE Vulnerability (CVE-2025-6514)," July 2025, https://jfrog.com/blog/2025-6514-critical-mcp-remote-rce-vulnerability/; GitHub Advisory GHSA-6xpm-ggf7-wc3p; patched in version 0.1.16). **CVE-2025-49596** (CVSS 9.4) was a remote code execution vulnerability in Anthropic's MCP Inspector, a developer debugging tool with 38,000+ weekly downloads and 4,000+ GitHub stars. Discovered by Oligo Security, the vulnerability exploited permissive CORS configuration and the browser treatment of `0.0.0.0` as localhost to achieve arbitrary command execution—simply by having a developer visit a malicious webpage while MCP Inspector was running (Oligo Security, "Critical RCE Vulnerability in Anthropic MCP Inspector (CVE-2025-49596)," 2025, https://www.oligo.security/blog/critical-rce-vulnerability-in-anthropic-mcp-inspector-cve-2025-49596; GitHub Advisory GHSA-7f8r-222p-6f5g; patched in version 0.14.1).

**Tool poisoning: weaponizing the protocol itself.** Perhaps the most distinctive class of MCP vulnerability is tool poisoning, first documented by Invariant Labs in April 2025 (Luca Beurer-Kellner and Marc Fischer, "MCP Security Notification: Tool Poisoning Attacks," Invariant Labs Blog, April 1, 2025, https://invariantlabs.ai/blog/mcp-security-notification-tool-poisoning-attacks; code at https://github.com/invariantlabs-ai/mcp-injection-experiments). In a tool poisoning attack, malicious instructions are embedded within MCP tool descriptions using `<IMPORTANT>` tags that are **invisible to users** in the simplified client UI but fully visible to the LLM. A seemingly innocent `add()` tool's hidden description can instruct the LLM to read `~/.cursor/mcp.json` and `~/.ssh/id_rsa` and transmit the data via hidden parameters. CyberArk Labs extended this to "Full-Schema Poisoning" (FSP) and "Advanced Tool Poisoning Attacks" (ATPA), demonstrating that the attack surface extends beyond description fields to parameter names, types, required arrays, and even tool *outputs* (Simcha Kosman, "Poison everywhere: No output from your MCP server is safe," CyberArk Threat Research Blog, 2025). The MCPTox benchmark empirically validated the severity: across 45 live MCP servers, 353 tools, and 1,312 malicious test cases evaluated against 20 LLM agents, **o1-mini exhibited a 72.8% attack success rate**, with GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-R1, and Phi-4 all exceeding 60%, and the highest refusal rate among all tested agents (Claude-3.7-Sonnet) was below 3% (Zhiqiang Wang et al., "MCPTox: A Benchmark for Tool Poisoning Attack on Real-World MCP Servers," arXiv:2508.14925, August 2025).

**Cross-server composition attacks: emergent vulnerabilities from individually safe servers.** When multiple MCP servers are connected to the same client, **individually safe servers become vulnerable through shared agent context**. Invariant Labs documented three composition attack patterns. *Tool shadowing* occurs when a malicious server injects a tool description that modifies agent behavior with respect to *other* servers' tools—without the malicious tool ever being invoked. In their demonstration, a malicious `add()` tool's hidden description redirected all emails sent via a trusted `send_email` tool to the attacker's address. *Rug pull attacks* exploit the fact that MCP clients treat tool definitions as static after initial approval: a server presents benign tool definitions during setup, then changes them to malicious ones on subsequent loads. Their `whatsapp-takeover.py` demonstration combined both techniques—a "random fact of the day" server that, on second load, transformed into a tool that exfiltrated complete WhatsApp chat histories via a legitimate WhatsApp MCP server (Invariant Labs, "WhatsApp MCP Exploited: Exfiltrating your message history via MCP," April 7, 2025, https://invariantlabs.ai/blog/whatsapp-mcp-exploited). *Cross-server data leakage* is formalized by Croce and South, who demonstrated that a minimal malicious weather MCP server could discover and exploit legitimate banking tools to steal account balances—requiring no backend infrastructure, no credentials, and no authentication bypass, only the shared agent context (Nicola Croce and Tobin South, "Trivial Trojans: How Minimal MCP Servers Enable Cross-Tool Exfiltration of Sensitive Data," arXiv:2507.19880, July 2025). Bhatt et al. proposed ETDI (Enhanced Tool Definition Integrity), incorporating cryptographic identity verification and immutable versioned tool definitions as a mitigation (arXiv:2506.01333, June 2025).

Trail of Bits' extensive blog series documented additional vectors: "line jumping"—where malicious MCP servers inject prompts through tool descriptions that manipulate AI behavior *before tools are ever invoked*, bypassing invocation controls entirely (Trail of Bits, "Jumping the line: How MCP servers can attack you before you ever use them," April 21, 2025, https://blog.trailofbits.com/2025/04/21/); trigger-phrase-based exfiltration of entire conversation histories (April 23, 2025); and ANSI terminal escape sequences hiding malicious payloads in tool descriptions (April 29, 2025). Their pajaMAS framework demonstrated multi-agent system (MAS) hijacking through Simon Willison's "lethal trifecta"—private data access combined with untrusted content and external communication capability (Trail of Bits, "Hijacking multi-agent systems in your PajaMAS," July 31, 2025).

**Ecosystem-level cataloguing and governance.** Multiple organizations have attempted to systematize these findings. Adversa AI launched the MCP Security TOP 25 Framework in September 2025, the "industry's first comprehensive resource" for cataloguing MCP vulnerabilities, threats, and defenses, covering prompt injection, rug pulls, tool poisoning, data leakage, multi-agent compromise, and classical injection flaws (Adversa AI, "MCP Security TOP 25," September 18, 2025, https://adversa.ai/mcp-security-top-25-mcp-vulnerabilities/). Their March 2026 digest reported **30 CVEs filed in 60 days** and found **38% of 500+ scanned servers completely lacked authentication**. Elastic Security Labs published a comprehensive analysis covering obfuscated instructions, rug-pull redefinitions, cross-tool orchestration, and passive influence, concluding that "MCP tools have a broad attack surface, as docstrings, parameter names, and external artifacts can all override agent behavior" (Carolina Beretta, Gus Carlock, and Andrew Pease, "MCP Tools: Attack Vectors and Defense Recommendations for Autonomous Agents," Elastic Security Labs, September 19, 2025, https://www.elastic.co/security-labs/mcp-tools-attack-defense-recommendations). Wiz's research briefing highlighted that MCP server registries offer "only weak or inconsistent trust signals" and that roughly a hundred of 3,500 listed servers linked to non-existent repositories (Rami McCarthy, "MCP Security: The present and future of security for the Model Context Protocol," Wiz Blog, April 17, 2025, https://www.wiz.io/blog/mcp-security-research-briefing).

## 3.3.4 The current attack-defense landscape

### 3.3.4.1 Quantifying the adversarial asymmetry

The empirical evidence converges on a sobering assessment of current defenses. The International AI Safety Report's Second Key Update—authored by Yoshua Bengio et al. and commissioned by 30 nations from the 2023 AI Safety Summit—states explicitly: "The success rate of prompt injection attacks, as reported by AI developers, **has been falling slightly over time, but when given ten tries, attackers can still successfully execute such attacks about half the time**" (Yoshua Bengio et al., *International AI Safety Report 2025: Second Key Update: Technical Safeguards and Risk Management*, October 15, 2025, arXiv:2510.13653; see also the 2026 edition published February 3, 2026, at https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/). The report adopts a "Swiss cheese model" for AI defense—each layer is imperfect, but layering reduces the probability of complete penetration—while noting that "the cost of circumventing safeguards is decreasing relative to the cost of developing and maintaining them."

Complementary quantitative evidence reinforces this picture. Cisco and the University of Pennsylvania found that DeepSeek R1 achieved **100% attack success rate** against 50 HarmBench prompts, failing to block a single one; Meta Llama 3.1 reached 96% (Cisco, "Evaluating Security Risk in DeepSeek and Other Frontier Reasoning Models," January 2025, https://blogs.cisco.com/security/evaluating-security-risk-in-deepseek-and-other-frontier-reasoning-models). In agentic settings, the MCPTox benchmark showed attack success rates up to 72.8% through tool poisoning alone, with agent refusal rates below 3%. A systematic analysis of complete computer takeover attacks found 94.4% susceptibility to direct injection, 83.3% to RAG backdoor attacks, and **100% to inter-agent communication exploitation**—revealing a particularly alarming "AI agent privilege escalation" where LLMs that resist human-origin injections execute identical payloads when requested by peer agents ("The Dark Side of LLMs: Agent-based Attacks for Complete Computer Takeover," arXiv:2507.06850, July 2025). The Tensor Trust game, involving 563,349 attacks and 118,377 defenses from human players, found GPT-4 achieved only approximately 84% robustness to hijacking and 69% to extraction (Sam Toyer et al., "Tensor Trust: Interpretable Prompt Injection Attacks from an Online Game," *ICLR 2024*, arXiv:2311.01011).

The HackAPrompt competition—collecting 600,000+ adversarial prompts across 29 distinct attack techniques—reached a decisive conclusion: **"prompt-based defenses do not work"** (Sander Schulhoff et al., "Ignore This Title and HackAPrompt: Exposing Systemic Vulnerabilities of LLMs Through a Global Scale Prompt Hacking Competition," *EMNLP 2023*, pp. 4945–4977, Best Theme Paper Award, arXiv:2311.16119). Microsoft's LLMail-Inject competition achieved more favorable numbers with maximal defense layering—combining spotlighting, Prompt Shield, LLM-as-judge, and TaskTracker—reducing end-to-end attack success to 0.8% in Phase 1 and 0.3% in Phase 2, but this required an all-out multi-layer defense architecture rarely deployed in production (Sahar Abdelnabi et al., "LLMail-Inject: A Dataset from a Realistic Adaptive Prompt Injection Challenge," arXiv:2506.09956, June 2025).

### 3.3.4.2 The structural impossibility of complete mitigation

The prompt injection problem is structurally different from classical software vulnerabilities like SQL injection or buffer overflows, where the vulnerability arises from implementation flaws fixable through input validation or memory-safe languages. Prompt injection arises from an **architectural property of LLMs themselves**: the absence of any principled separation between instructions and data at the token level.

This was formally quantified by Zverev et al. at ICLR 2025, who introduced the first formal measure of instruction-data separation in LLMs. Their findings were unequivocal: **all tested models fail to achieve high separation**. GPT-4 scored 0.225 on their separation metric (best among tested models), meaning it executed rather than merely processed the majority of probe strings intended as data. They further showed that "canonical mitigation techniques, such as prompt engineering and fine-tuning, either fail to substantially improve separation or reduce model utility" (Egor Zverev, Sahar Abdelnabi, Soroush Tabesh, Mario Fritz, and Christoph H. Lampert, "Can LLMs Separate Instructions From Data? And What Do We Even Mean By That?," *ICLR 2025*, arXiv:2403.06833).

Industry leaders have publicly acknowledged this structural constraint. OpenAI's December 2025 blog post on Atlas security stated: **"Prompt injection, much like scams and social engineering on the web, is unlikely to ever be fully 'solved'"**, describing it as "a long-term AI security challenge" requiring continuous defense strengthening (OpenAI, "Continuously hardening ChatGPT Atlas against prompt injection attacks," December 22, 2025). The UK National Cyber Security Centre independently warned in the same month: "As there is no inherent distinction between 'data' and 'instruction', it's very possible that prompt injection attacks may never be totally mitigated in the way that SQL injection attacks can be" (David C., "Prompt injection is not SQL injection (it may be worse)," UK NCSC Blog, December 8, 2025, https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blog-post/prompt-injection-is-not-sql-injection).

Schneier and Raghavan formalized the impossibility argument in the context of agentic AI's OODA (Observe-Orient-Decide-Act) loop: "Prompt injection is possible because an AI mixes untrusted inputs with trusted instructions and then confuses one for the other. This isn't just a filtering problem; it's architectural. LLMs process token sequences, but no mechanism exists to mark token privileges. Every solution proposed introduces new injection vectors" (Bruce Schneier and Barath Raghavan, "Agentic AI's OODA Loop Problem," *IEEE Security & Privacy*, October 2025). The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025, which ranks prompt injection as **vulnerability #1** for the second consecutive edition, concurs: "Given the stochastic influence at the heart of the way models work, it is unclear if there are fool-proof methods of prevention for prompt injection" (OWASP Foundation, "OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications 2025," v2025, https://genai.owasp.org/llm-top-10/).

### 3.3.4.3 The defense landscape in brief

Current defenses, which are examined in detail in Section 5, operate along several axes. *Input/output filtering* deploys classifier models to detect injection attempts before they reach the primary LLM—Microsoft's Prompt Shields and Azure AI Content Safety represent the most widely deployed production implementations. *Instruction hierarchy* trains models to prioritize system-level instructions over user inputs and third-party content, with OpenAI demonstrating 63% improvement against system prompt extraction on GPT-3.5 (Eric Wallace et al., "The Instruction Hierarchy: Training LLMs to Prioritize Privileged Instructions," arXiv:2404.13208, 2024) and Wu et al. proposing architectural-level Instructional Segment Embeddings achieving up to 15.75% improvement in robust accuracy against IPI (*ICLR 2025*). *Spotlighting and delimiters* use data encoding transformations to help LLMs distinguish between trusted and untrusted content, reducing attack success from over 50% to below 2% in controlled experiments—though adaptive attacks have demonstrated bypass capability (Keegan Hines et al., "Defending Against Indirect Prompt Injection Attacks With Spotlighting," arXiv:2403.14720, 2024). *Dual-LLM architectures*, exemplified by Google DeepMind's CaMeL framework, treat the primary LLM as an untrusted element within a secure pipeline, explicitly separating privileged command execution from content processing. *Structured queries* (StruQ) enforce formal separation of prompts and data through format restrictions (Sizhe Chen et al., "StruQ: Defending Against Prompt Injection with Structured Queries," *USENIX Security 2025*, arXiv:2402.06363).

None of these defenses is individually sufficient. Nasr et al., in a joint study across researchers at OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind, tested 12 published defenses using adaptive attack methods—gradient descent, reinforcement learning, random search, and human red teaming. Defenses that originally reported near-zero attack success rates were **bypassed with attack success rates above 90%** under adaptive conditions. The LLMail-Inject competition achieved its low attack success rates only through maximal defense stacking—a configuration that introduces significant latency, cost, and utility trade-offs. The emerging consensus, reflected in the International AI Safety Report's adoption of the Swiss cheese model, is that defense-in-depth with continuous adversarial hardening represents the only viable strategy—a perpetual arms race rather than a problem admitting definitive solution.

The escalation narrative traced in this section—from direct attacks on chatbots, to indirect attacks on RAG systems, to weaponized attacks on tool-using agents, to ecosystem-level vulnerabilities in the MCP integration layer—represents not merely an expansion of the attack surface but a qualitative transformation in the threat model. Each step grants the attacker more leverage, broader reach, and more consequential impact, while the fundamental architectural vulnerability remains unchanged.
