# [C11] §4.2 Industry Self-Regulation, Platform Governance, and the Agentic AI Governance Gap

The AI industry's self-regulatory apparatus has expanded rapidly since 2023, yet remains structurally insufficient for the agentic systems now entering production. Twelve frontier AI companies published safety frameworks by early 2025, major platforms adopted AI content labeling regimes, and international alliances secured voluntary commitments from dozens of governments and firms. Despite this institutional activity, the Future of Life Institute's AI Safety Index found **no company scoring above a D in existential safety**, and the emergence of autonomous AI agents—products already generating billions in revenue—has exposed a governance vacuum that existing frameworks were never designed to fill. The gap between deployment velocity and governance maturity represents perhaps the most consequential regulatory failure in the current AI landscape.

---

## 4.2.1 Frontier AI safety frameworks: twelve companies, one deadline

The Seoul AI Safety Summit of May 21–22, 2024, established a pivotal deadline: sixteen companies signed the Frontier AI Safety Commitments, pledging to publish safety frameworks before the Paris AI Action Summit in February 2025. The result was a concentrated burst of institutional self-regulation. METR (Model Evaluation & Threat Research) documented exactly **twelve companies** that published frontier AI safety policies by December 2025: Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI, Microsoft, Amazon, NVIDIA, Cohere, G42, Naver, and Magic (METR, "Common Elements of Frontier AI Safety Policies," December 9, 2025, https://metr.org/blog/2025-12-09-common-elements-of-frontier-ai-safety-policies/).

All twelve frameworks share a common architecture: capability thresholds that trigger escalating safeguards, with risk domains spanning CBRN weapons, cybersecurity, and AI research automation. The frameworks diverge in specificity, governance mechanisms, and—critically—their treatment of agentic capabilities.

**Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP)**, now in version 3.0 (February 24, 2026), is the most mature and frequently revised framework. It establishes AI Safety Levels (ASL-1 through ASL-4+), modeled loosely on biosafety levels. ASL-3 was activated in May 2025 for models posing CBRN-related deployment risks, requiring robust jailbreak resistance and enhanced security against state-level actors. Crucially, RSP v3.0 explicitly acknowledges the agentic transition, noting that "a two-year-old policy written for chatbots is applied to systems that can browse the internet, write and execute code, and perform multi-step tasks over extended periods" (Anthropic, "Responsible Scaling Policy v3.0," February 24, 2026, https://anthropic.com/responsible-scaling-policy/rsp-v3-0). However, v3.0 also drew criticism from safety advocates for removing the hard commitment to pause training if safeguards proved inadequate, replacing it with a dual condition requiring both that Anthropic leads the AI race and that material catastrophic risk exists.

**OpenAI's Preparedness Framework**, updated to version 2.0 on April 15, 2025, streamlines risk tracking to two thresholds: "High" (amplifying existing harm pathways, requiring safeguards before deployment) and "Critical" (introducing unprecedented harm pathways, requiring safeguards during development). The framework evaluates biological/chemical and cybersecurity capabilities as tracked categories, with model autonomy relegated to a "Research Category" not yet formally tracked. Notably, persuasion risk was **removed entirely from v2.0**, handled instead through OpenAI's Model Spec—a decision that drew criticism from safety researchers (OpenAI, "Preparedness Framework v2.0," April 15, 2025, https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/18a02b5d-6b67-4cec-ab64-68cdfbddebcd/preparedness-framework-v2.pdf).

**Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework (FSF)**, reaching version 3.0 by September 22, 2025, introduced the most granular treatment of misalignment risk among the frameworks. It defines Critical Capability Levels (CCLs) spanning misuse risk, ML R&D risk, and misalignment risk—including explicit evaluations for deceptive alignment, instrumental reasoning, and situational awareness. Version 3.0 added a new CCL for "harmful manipulation," addressing AI systems with capabilities to systematically alter beliefs or behaviors at scale (Google DeepMind, "Strengthening Our Frontier Safety Framework," September 22, 2025, https://deepmind.google/blog/strengthening-our-frontier-safety-framework/).

**Meta's Frontier AI Framework** (February 3, 2025) takes a distinctive "outcomes-led approach," evaluating whether a model uniquely enables threat scenarios leading to catastrophic outcomes rather than measuring raw capability. **Microsoft's Frontier Governance Framework** (February 8, 2025) explicitly tracks "Advanced Autonomy" as a capability category, with thresholds for models that can "autonomously complete a range of generalist tasks equivalent to multiple days' worth of generalist human labor." **Amazon's Frontier Model Safety Framework** (February 9–10, 2025) emphasizes quantifying "uplift"—whether a model materially increases an actor's ability to cause harm compared to existing tools. **xAI's Risk Management Framework**, published as a draft on February 20, 2025, and finalized August 20, 2025, received significant criticism from organizations including AI Lab Watch for its reliance on narrow benchmarks and minimal safety staffing.

The February 2025 publication wave—with Meta, Google DeepMind, G42, Cohere, Microsoft, Amazon, and NVIDIA all publishing within a two-week window—reveals these frameworks' dual nature as both genuine safety commitments and compliance documents designed to meet an externally imposed deadline. All remain **voluntary, self-enforced, and subject to self-assessed thresholds**, with no independent verification mechanism.

## 4.2.2 The FLI AI Safety Index reveals an industry earning C grades

The Future of Life Institute's AI Safety Index provides the most systematic external assessment of corporate AI safety practices. Published in three editions—November 2024 (inaugural), July 2025 (Summer), and December 2025 (Winter)—the index evaluates frontier AI companies across six domains using expert panels and a US GPA-style grading system (Future of Life Institute, "AI Safety Index Winter 2025," December 2025, https://futureoflife.org/ai-safety-index-winter-2025/).

The Winter 2025 edition, evaluated by an eight-member expert panel including Stuart Russell (UC Berkeley), David Krueger (Mila/University of Montreal), and Dylan Hadfield-Menell (MIT), assessed eight companies across **35 indicators in six domains**: Risk Assessment, Current Harms, Safety Frameworks, Existential Safety, Governance & Accountability, and Information Sharing. **Anthropic ranked first with a C+ (2.67)**, followed by **OpenAI at C+ (2.31)** and Google DeepMind at C (2.08). The remaining five companies—xAI, Z.ai, Meta, DeepSeek, and Alibaba Cloud—all received D or D- grades, revealing a stark two-tier divide in the industry.

The most striking finding, consistent across both 2025 editions, is that **no company scored above a D in the Existential Safety domain**. Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google DeepMind each received a D; xAI, Z.ai, Meta, DeepSeek, and Alibaba Cloud all received F grades. This domain evaluates four indicators: existential safety strategy, internal monitoring and control interventions, technical AI safety research, and support for external safety research. The report concluded that "all of the companies reviewed are racing toward AGI/superintelligence without presenting any explicit plans for controlling or aligning such smarter-than-human technology, thus leaving the most consequential risks effectively unaddressed." Stuart Russell stated bluntly: "AI CEOs claim they know how to build superhuman AI, yet none can show how they'll prevent us from losing control—after which humanity's survival is no longer in our hands" (FLI, Winter 2025 Full Report, https://futureoflife.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/AI-Safety-Index-Report_011225_Full_Report_Digital.pdf).

Anthropic's relative leadership stems from stronger performance in Information Sharing (A-), Risk Assessment (B), and Governance & Accountability (B-). OpenAI improved from C to C+ between the Summer and Winter editions, narrowing the gap with Anthropic. Max Tegmark, FLI president, captured the broader context: "The only reason that there are so many C's and D's and F's in the report is because there are fewer regulations on AI than on making sandwiches."

## 4.2.3 Red teaming has matured from ad hoc exercises to institutional practice

Red teaming at frontier AI labs has evolved from early crowdworker experiments into a multi-layered system combining human expertise, automated tools, and external programs. The practice now operates across three distinct modalities, each with characteristic strengths and limitations.

**Human red teaming** remains the gold standard for discovering novel attack vectors. Anthropic's foundational work (Ganguli et al., "Red Teaming Language Models to Reduce Harms," August 2022, arXiv:2209.07858) established methods for structured adversarial testing using crowdworkers, producing a dataset of **38,961 red team attacks** released publicly. By 2025, Anthropic's Frontier Red Team had evolved to evaluate catastrophic risk capabilities in partnership with the U.S. National Nuclear Security Administration and the UK and US AI Safety Institutes (Anthropic, "Progress from our Frontier Red Team," 2025, https://www.anthropic.com/news/strategic-warning-for-ai-risk-progress-and-insights-from-our-frontier-red-team). OpenAI formalized its approach through the Red Teaming Network (September 2023), a standing bench of domain experts in cybersecurity, natural sciences, and regional politics, complemented by a detailed white paper on methodology (Ahmad et al., "OpenAI's Approach to External Red Teaming," March 2025, arXiv:2503.16431). Google established a dedicated AI Red Team leveraging intelligence from Mandiant and its Threat Analysis Group (Google, "Google's AI Red Team: The Ethical Hackers Making AI Safer," July 19, 2023, https://blog.google/technology/safety-security/googles-ai-red-team-the-ethical-hackers-making-ai-safer/). Meta introduced GOAT (Generative Offensive Agent Tester) with Llama 4 in April 2025, automating multi-turn adversarial interactions that simulate medium-skilled attackers.

**Automated red teaming tools** have become essential for scalability. Microsoft's PyRIT (Python Risk Identification Tool), open-sourced February 22, 2024, has been used in over 100 red teaming operations, generating thousands of adversarial prompts and evaluating outputs in hours rather than weeks (Microsoft, "Announcing Microsoft's Open Automation Framework to Red Team Generative AI Systems," February 22, 2024, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2024/02/22/announcing-microsofts-open-automation-framework-to-red-team-generative-ai-systems/). NVIDIA's Garak vulnerability scanner provides open-source LLM security probing (Derczynski et al., "garak: A Framework for Security Probing Large Language Models," June 2024, arXiv:2406.11036). Google's internal Automated Red Teaming program continuously attacks Gemini models, reportedly achieving significant improvements in protection against indirect prompt injection during tool use. The pioneering academic work by Perez et al. at DeepMind ("Red Teaming Language Models with Language Models," EMNLP 2022, arXiv:2202.03286) demonstrated the viability of using one language model to systematically discover failures in another.

**External bug bounty programs** extend the security perimeter beyond company walls. OpenAI launched its bug bounty on Bugcrowd in April 2023, increasing maximum payouts from $20,000 to **$100,000 for critical findings** in March 2025—though model safety issues such as jailbreaks remain explicitly out of scope. Google expanded its Vulnerability Rewards Program to cover generative AI attack scenarios in October 2023, awarding over $430,000 for AI-related findings, with a dedicated AI VRP launching in October 2025 (Google Bug Hunters, "Announcing Google's New AI Vulnerability Reward Program," October 2025, https://bughunters.google.com/blog/announcing-googles-new-ai-vulnerability-reward-program). The most ambitious external exercise was the DEF CON 31 Generative Red Team Challenge (August 10–13, 2023), where **2,244 hackers evaluated eight LLMs** from Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI, and others, producing over 17,000 adversarial conversations—supported by the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy and organized by AI Village and Humane Intelligence (AI Village, "AI Village at DEF CON Announces Largest-Ever Public Generative AI Red Team," May 3, 2023, https://aivillage.org/generative%20red%20team/2023/05/03/generative-red-team).

## 4.2.4 Acceptable use policies diverge sharply on enforceability

All major AI providers maintain acceptable use policies (AUPs) prohibiting broadly similar categories of harmful use—CSAM, weapons development, harassment, deception, and unauthorized surveillance. The substantive differences lie not in what is prohibited but in **how prohibitions can be enforced**, a distinction driven primarily by the open-weight versus API-only divide.

API-only providers—OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google—maintain comprehensive runtime monitoring. OpenAI's Usage Policies (last updated October 29, 2025, https://openai.com/policies/usage-policies/) operate through a layered hierarchy: root-level safety training baked into the model via the Model Spec, platform-level automated detection and human review, and developer-facing moderation tools. OpenAI has disrupted more than 40 malicious networks since February 2024. Anthropic's Usage Policy (updated September 15, 2025, https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup) consolidated former separate prohibited use lists into unified "Universal Usage Standards" and introduced specific provisions for agentic AI use cases. Google's Generative AI Prohibited Use Policy (refreshed December 17, 2024, https://policies.google.com/terms/generative-ai/use-policy) is notable for its brevity, organized around just four categories, and includes a distinctive exception clause for educational, documentary, scientific, or artistic purposes.

The enforceability picture changes fundamentally for open-weight providers. **Meta's Llama AUP** (https://www.llama.com/llama4/use-policy/) is incorporated into the Llama Community License Agreement—violation theoretically triggers license termination. But once model weights are downloaded, no technical enforcement mechanism exists. Meta cannot monitor usage on user infrastructure; enforcement becomes a legal instrument rather than a technical one. Mistral AI's policy goes further: its Usage Policy (https://legal.mistral.ai/terms/usage-policy) applies only to products hosted on Mistral's own platform and "does not apply to Mistral AI Products deployed on a customer's infrastructure, on the infrastructure of our partners, or to our open-source AI models and products." This makes Mistral's open-source models **the least policy-constrained offerings from any major provider**.

## 4.2.5 Model cards and system cards: transparency with structural limits

The model card concept, introduced by Mitchell et al. in "Model Cards for Model Reporting" (FAT* 2019, arXiv:1810.03993), proposed standardized documentation covering intended use, limitations, evaluation results, and bias assessments. Every major frontier AI company now publishes some form of model documentation, though formats, depth, and nomenclature vary considerably.

OpenAI has published system cards for GPT-4 (March 2023), GPT-4o (August 2024, arXiv:2410.21276), GPT-4.5 (February 2025), and a ChatGPT Agent System Card (July 2025). The GPT-4o card involved **100+ external red teamers across 45 languages and 29 countries**, with third-party assessments by METR and Apollo Research on autonomous capabilities. Anthropic has progressively shifted from "model cards" (Claude 3, March 2024, https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/de8ba9b01c9ab7cbabf5c33b80b7bbc618857627/Model_Card_Claude_3.pdf) to "system cards" (Claude 3.7 Sonnet onward, https://anthropic.com/claude-3-7-sonnet-system-card), reflecting broader system-level analysis including deployment context, prompt injection risks, and agentic behaviors. Google maintains both comprehensive technical reports per model family and regularly updated model cards per specific version through its Model Cards portal (https://modelcards.withgoogle.com/). Meta publishes Llama model cards directly on GitHub alongside model code and weights, including training energy use and carbon footprint data.

The limitations of self-reporting are well-documented and structural. Mitchell et al. acknowledged at the outset that "the usefulness and accuracy of a model card relies on the integrity of the creator(s) of the card itself." An analysis of 100 Hugging Face model cards found that interpretability was detailed in fewer than 20% of cards, bias assessments rarely achieved depth, and "documentation often does not evolve as models evolve" (AI Transparency Atlas, arXiv:2512.12443). OpenAI's GPT-4 System Card explicitly declined to share information about dataset construction "due to the competitive landscape and safety implications." A proposed reporting standard (STREAM, arXiv:2508.09853) found that model reports frequently omit basic methodological details and present results of privately developed evaluations with limited documentation. Usage information across model cards appears under **97 different section names**, demonstrating severe inconsistency. These documents remain, fundamentally, marketing-adjacent artifacts controlled entirely by the publishing company, with no independent audit requirement.

## 4.2.6 Platform governance for AI-generated content: labeling as the primary strategy

Major platforms have converged on **labeling rather than removal** as their primary governance mechanism for AI-generated content—a significant philosophical shift from earlier content moderation paradigms.

**Meta** has iterated through multiple labeling regimes since 2023. Initial "Imagined with AI" watermarks on Meta AI-generated images gave way to broader "Made with AI" labels in May 2024, using C2PA and IPTC metadata to detect AI-generated images from third-party tools including Google, OpenAI, Microsoft, and Adobe. After photographers complained about overly broad application—retouched images triggering labels due to residual metadata from tools like Photoshop—Meta revised the label to the more ambiguous "AI Info" in July 2024. Concurrently, Meta stopped removing content solely under its manipulated video policy, preferring labeling plus enforcement only when content violates other Community Standards. For advertising, Meta requires political and social issue advertisers to disclose AI-generated photorealistic content since January 1, 2024, and prohibits use of its generative AI ad tools for political ads entirely (Clegg, "Labeling AI-Generated Images on Facebook, Instagram and Threads," February 6, 2024, https://about.fb.com/news/2024/02/labeling-ai-generated-images-on-facebook-instagram-and-threads/).

**Google/YouTube** deploys SynthID, a watermarking technology developed by Google DeepMind that embeds imperceptible signals directly into AI-generated content. Initially launched in August 2023 for images, SynthID expanded to audio, text, and video by 2024, with over **10 billion pieces of content watermarked** by May 2025. SynthID's limitation is ecosystem specificity—it detects content from Google's AI tools but cannot identify AI content from other providers. YouTube introduced mandatory disclosure tools in Creator Studio (March 18, 2024), requiring creators to label realistic synthetic content. By July 2025, YouTube began implementing stricter monetization policies targeting mass-produced AI content, requiring output to be "significantly original and authentic" (YouTube Official Blog, "How We're Helping Creators Disclose Altered or Synthetic Content," March 18, 2024, https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content/).

**TikTok** has emerged as the most restrictive platform. Its AI-generated content label launched September 19, 2023, with automatic detection using C2PA Content Credentials and proprietary invisible watermarks added by May 2024. TikTok completely bans AI-generated political advertisements and prohibits AI likenesses of minors and non-consenting adults. The platform reported removing 51,000 synthetic media videos for policy violations and labeling over **1.3 billion AI-generated videos** (TikTok Newsroom, "New Labels for Disclosing AI-Generated Content," September 19, 2023, https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/new-labels-for-disclosing-ai-generated-content).

**X (formerly Twitter)** represents the opposite end of the spectrum. Its Synthetic and Manipulated Media Policy, introduced February 4, 2020, has seen no significant AI-specific updates. X relies primarily on Community Notes—a crowd-sourced fact-checking system where research shows fewer than 9% of submitted notes reach the general audience. X has no mandatory automated labeling system and no specific advertiser AI rules. Its own AI chatbot Grok has generated controversy for producing nonconsensual deepfake imagery, creating a situation where the platform's AI product actively undermines its content governance.

## 4.2.7 C2PA and Content Credentials are approaching critical adoption mass

The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA), founded in February 2021 by Adobe, Arm, BBC, Intel, Microsoft, and Truepic, has achieved remarkable institutional adoption as an open standard for cryptographically signed content provenance metadata. Operating as a project of the Joint Development Foundation under the Linux Foundation, C2PA unifies two predecessor initiatives: Adobe's Content Authenticity Initiative (founded 2019) and Microsoft/BBC's Project Origin (https://c2pa.org/about/).

The C2PA specification, currently at version 2.2 (May 1, 2025), embeds tamper-evident "Content Credentials" into media files using X.509 certificates and PKI infrastructure. Adoption has accelerated across three vectors. **Hardware manufacturers** have embedded C2PA directly: Leica's M11-P (October 2023) was the world's first consumer camera with built-in support; Sony, Nikon, and Canon followed; and the Google Pixel 10 (September 2025) became the first smartphone signing every photo by default with hardware-backed keys. **AI tools** including Adobe Firefly, OpenAI's DALL-E 3 and Sora, and Microsoft Designer embed Content Credentials in generated outputs. **Platforms** including Meta, Google, YouTube, TikTok, and LinkedIn detect C2PA metadata for labeling purposes. The Content Authenticity Initiative has grown to over **5,000 members**, and the steering committee now includes Adobe, Amazon, BBC, Google, Intel, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Sony, and Truepic.

CISA endorsed Content Credentials in a January 2025 advisory, "Strengthening Multimedia Integrity in the Generative AI Era," recommending adoption by government agencies. C2PA is on track for ISO standardization. Cloudflare's implementation brings Content Credentials infrastructure to approximately 20% of web traffic. Despite this momentum, fundamental challenges persist: metadata can be stripped during sharing, the standard cannot authenticate content that was never enrolled, and adoption across the global content ecosystem remains uneven.

## 4.2.8 Industry alliances and voluntary commitments created an institutional scaffolding

A dense network of industry alliances and multilateral commitments provides the institutional scaffolding for AI self-regulation, though their voluntary nature limits enforceability.

The **Partnership on AI (PAI)**, founded September 28, 2016, by Amazon, Facebook, Google, DeepMind, Microsoft, and IBM, has grown to **126 partner organizations across 16 countries** (https://partnershiponai.org/). Its five program areas—AI and Media Integrity, AI Work and Economy, Justice and Accountability, Inclusive Research and Design, and Security for AI—have produced frameworks including the Synthetic Media Framework (February 2023) and guidance for safe foundation model deployment.

The **Frontier Model Forum (FMF)**, announced July 26, 2023, by Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI (later joined by Amazon and Meta), operates as a 501(c)(6) nonprofit focused on frontier AI safety standards and research. Its AI Safety Fund exceeds **$10 million** for independent research, supported by founding members and philanthropic partners including Schmidt Sciences and the Patrick J. McGovern Foundation (https://www.frontiermodelforum.org/).

The **White House Voluntary AI Commitments** (July 21, 2023) secured eight specific pledges from seven initial companies—Amazon, Anthropic, Google, Inflection, Meta, Microsoft, and OpenAI—with eight additional companies joining in September 2023 (Adobe, Cohere, IBM, NVIDIA, Palantir, Salesforce, Scale AI, Stability AI). Commitments covered internal and external security testing, information sharing, cybersecurity investment, vulnerability reporting, watermarking, public capability reporting, societal risk research, and beneficial AI development (White House, "FACT SHEET: Biden-Harris Administration Secures Voluntary Commitments," July 21, 2023, https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/2023/07/21/fact-sheet-biden-harris-administration-secures-voluntary-commitments-from-leading-artificial-intelligence-companies-to-manage-the-risks-posed-by-ai/). A one-year assessment by MIT Technology Review found companies were "nowhere near where we need them to be in terms of good governance," with uncertain additionality—it was unclear whether companies would have implemented the same measures regardless of the commitments.

The **Seoul AI Safety Summit** (May 21–22, 2024) produced the Seoul Declaration signed by ten countries plus the EU, the Frontier AI Safety Commitments from sixteen companies, and a statement of intent toward international cooperation on AI safety science that catalyzed the **International Network of AI Safety Institutes**. This network, inaugurated in San Francisco in November 2024, includes safety institutes in the UK, US, Japan, Singapore, Korea, and the EU. The **OECD AI Principles**, first adopted May 22, 2019, and updated May 3, 2024, remain the foundational intergovernmental standard—now adhered to by **47 governments** and endorsed by the G20, shaping national AI strategies worldwide (https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles).

The **Bletchley Declaration** (November 1–2, 2023) achieved a milestone as the first global AI safety agreement with 28 countries plus the EU—notably including China—while the **G7 Hiroshima AI Process** produced both International Guiding Principles and an eleven-action Code of Conduct for AI developers (October 30, 2023). The **Paris AI Action Summit** (February 10–11, 2025) continued this trajectory but marked a noticeable shift toward innovation and competition framing, with the US and UK declining to sign the joint statement.

## 4.2.9 Agentic AI has exposed a structural governance vacuum

The governance gap for agentic AI is not merely a failure of speed—it is a structural mismatch between the paradigm assumed by existing frameworks and the paradigm of deployed systems. Current frontier safety frameworks, the NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, and the EU AI Act contain **zero references to "agent," "agentic," or autonomous AI systems** (Zenity, "The Agentic AI Governance Blind Spot," 2025, https://zenity.io/blog/security/the-agentic-ai-governance-blind-spot-why-the-leading-frameworks-are-already-outdated). As McKinsey's security playbook observed: "Enterprise cybersecurity frameworks—such as ISO 27001, NIST CSF, and SOC 2—focus on systems, processes, and people. They do not yet fully account for autonomous agents that can act with discretion and adaptability."

AI agents introduce qualitatively different risks from chat-based models: autonomous real-world action execution, dynamic tool access across APIs and file systems, multi-step planning with cascading failure potential, persistent memory vulnerable to manipulation, and multi-agent delegation creating accountability fragmentation. The 2025 AI Agent Index documented **30 deployed agentic AI products**, finding "foundation model concentration, accountability fragmentation, and capability-safety transparency gaps" across the ecosystem (arXiv:2602.17753v1).

The deployment timeline is stark. Anthropic launched Computer Use in public beta on October 22, 2024—the first frontier AI model capable of autonomous desktop control. OpenAI's Operator browser agent followed on January 23, 2025. Claude Code reached general availability on May 22, 2025, generating **$1 billion in annualized recurring revenue by November 2025**. OpenAI's Codex launched May 16, 2025, accumulating 1.6 million weekly active users by March 2026. Cognition's Devin, first announced March 12, 2024, went public December 10, 2024. Google's Project Mariner, Salesforce Agentforce, and Microsoft Copilot Actions all entered production in 2024–2025. None of these products launched under any agent-specific governance standard—they operated under general terms of service, company-specific safety testing, and enterprise security frameworks that were never designed for autonomous systems. The real-world consequences became vivid in November 2025, when Anthropic disclosed that a Chinese state-sponsored threat actor ("GTG-1002") had used Claude Code to orchestrate what it described as the **first reported AI-orchestrated cyber espionage campaign**, with AI executing 80–90% of tactical operations autonomously against approximately 30 organizations.

## 4.2.10 MCP's security evolution illustrates the governance-first failure pattern

Anthropic's Model Context Protocol (MCP), launched in November 2024 as an open standard for connecting AI systems to external tools and data sources, exemplifies the pattern of deploying capability first and retroactively addressing security. The original specification used "SHOULD" rather than "MUST" for security controls, making authentication and authorization voluntary rather than enforced. The spec explicitly stated: "While MCP itself cannot enforce these security principles at the protocol level, implementors SHOULD build robust consent and authorization flows into their applications" (MCP Specification v2025-11-25, https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-11-25).

The consequences were predictable. Over **13,000 MCP servers launched on GitHub in 2025** alone, many deployed without authentication (Zenity, "Securing the Model Context Protocol," 2025, https://zenity.io/blog/security/securing-the-model-context-protocol-mcp). Security researchers identified multiple vulnerabilities: prompt injection through tool descriptions, tool-poisoning attacks where malicious servers silently replace trusted tools, data exfiltration through tool-chaining, and the "confused deputy" problem where the LLM is manipulated into unauthorized actions (Red Hat, "Model Context Protocol: Understanding Security Risks and Controls," 2025, https://www.redhat.com/en/blog/model-context-protocol-mcp-understanding-security-risks-and-controls). A CVE (CVE-2025-49596) was discovered in Anthropic's own MCP Inspector, enabling browser-based attacks leading to remote code execution.

MCP's security posture has improved iteratively. The March 26, 2025 specification added OAuth 2.1 with PKCE; the June 18, 2025 specification classified MCP servers as OAuth 2.0 Resource Servers and added a dedicated Security Considerations section; and the November 2025 anniversary update introduced URL-based client registration and enhanced authorization flows, with AWS collaborating on authentication specifications (AWS Open Source Blog, "Authentication on MCP," 2025, https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/opensource/open-protocols-for-agent-interoperability-part-2-authentication-on-mcp/). MCP was donated to the Linux Foundation in December 2025. Yet as AWS noted, significant gaps remain for autonomous agent scenarios where no human is in the loop.

## 4.2.11 Emerging standards are chasing a moving target

The first systematic attempt to codify agent-specific security risks came with the **OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications**, released December 9–10, 2025, by the OWASP GenAI Security Project's Agentic Security Initiative. Developed by over 100 security researchers with an Expert Review Board including representatives from NIST, the European Commission, the Alan Turing Institute, Microsoft, and AWS, it identifies ten risks spanning Agent Goal Hijack (ASI01) through Rogue Agents (ASI10)—with three of the top four risks revolving around identities, tools, and delegated trust boundaries (OWASP, "Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026," https://genai.owasp.org/resource/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-applications-for-2026/). Companion resources include a Practical Guide for Securing Agentic Applications and, notably, "A Practical Guide for Secure MCP Server Development" (February 2026).

Security firms have mobilized rapidly. Palo Alto Networks mapped its Prisma AIRS product to all ten OWASP agentic risks (December 2025). The Cloud Security Alliance published the Agentic Trust Framework, applying Zero Trust principles to AI agents with four maturity levels—Intern through Principal (February 2, 2026, https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/blog/2026/02/02/the-agentic-trust-framework-zero-trust-governance-for-ai-agents). Zenity, Koi Security, Red Hat, and Astrix Security have published analyses of agentic AI security gaps, with Koi discovering malicious MCP servers including a Postmark impersonation and an npm backdoor.

Government standard-setting has begun but remains early-stage. **Singapore's IMDA published the world's first government governance framework specifically for agentic AI** on January 22, 2026, addressing risk bounding, human accountability, technical controls, and end-user responsibility (IMDA, "Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI," https://www.imda.gov.sg/-/media/imda/files/about/emerging-tech-and-research/artificial-intelligence/mgf-for-agentic-ai.pdf). **NIST launched its AI Agent Standards Initiative in February 2026**, structured around three pillars: industry-led standards, community-led open-source protocol development, and research in agent security and identity. A Request for Information published January 8, 2026, received 932 comments (NIST, "Announcing the AI Agent Standards Initiative," February 2026, https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/02/announcing-ai-agent-standards-initiative-interoperable-and-secure). NIST also initiated a concept paper on "AI Agent Identity and Authorization" through the National Cybersecurity Center of Excellence.

## Conclusion: the governance architecture is a generation behind the technology

The current self-regulatory landscape reveals a paradox. Institutional activity has been prodigious—twelve frontier safety frameworks, multiple platform labeling regimes, international summit declarations, industry alliances, and emerging OWASP standards. Yet this apparatus was designed for a previous paradigm: models that generate outputs reviewed by humans. The agentic transition—AI systems that autonomously execute actions, access tools, and operate persistently across sessions—has outpaced governance by **16 or more months**, with the first agent products shipping in October 2024 and the first government frameworks arriving only in January 2026.

Three structural weaknesses define the current moment. First, **no mandatory, certifiable standard equivalent to ISO 27001 exists for AI agents**; ISO/IEC 42001 addresses AI management systems generically but contains no agent-specific provisions. Second, the MCP governance trajectory—capability deployed first, security retrofitted months later—has become a template rather than an exception, with protocol adoption outrunning security review. Third, open-weight models compound the enforcement gap: Meta's Llama and Mistral's open-source releases carry AUPs that are unenforceable once weights are downloaded, and emerging agentic tools built on these models operate entirely outside any vendor oversight.

The FLI AI Safety Index's universal D and F grades in existential safety, combined with the absence of agent-specific governance, suggest that the industry's self-regulatory apparatus addresses the risks it has agreed to measure while systematically ignoring the risks that most challenge its business models—autonomous AI systems operating at scale with real-world consequence and no standardized accountability framework.
