# [C15] §6 Future Research Directions in AIGC Security

The rapid proliferation of AI-generated content has outpaced the security, safety, and governance mechanisms designed to manage it. Six critical research frontiers remain largely unsolved: securing autonomous AI agents against novel attack surfaces, building unified detection across content modalities, achieving provably robust watermarking, preserving privacy in generative models, harmonizing international governance regimes, and constructing evaluation frameworks that keep pace with model capabilities. This section maps each frontier's current gap, explains its significance, and identifies concrete research paths forward, anchored in the most recent work from 2023–2026.

---

## 6.1 Securing autonomous AI agents

**Current gap.** AI agents that autonomously invoke tools, access external data, and delegate tasks to other agents introduce attack surfaces that no existing security framework adequately addresses. OWASP's Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025 (v4.2.0, led by Steve Wilson et al., OWASP GenAI Security Project, 2025) identifies "Excessive Agency" (LLM06) as a top risk, and NIST's Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, Autio et al., July 2024) catalogs GAI-specific risks, but neither provides a complete security architecture for agentic workflows. NIST announced a dedicated AI Agent Standards Initiative in February 2026 (Federal Register 2026-00206), signaling recognition that current frameworks are insufficient. A comprehensive threat taxonomy was recently proposed by Deng et al. in "AI Agents Under Threat: A Survey of Key Security Challenges and Future Pathways" (ACM Computing Surveys, Vol. 57, No. 7, 2025), categorizing threats across unpredictable multi-step inputs, complex internal execution, variable operational environments, and untrusted external entities. Ferrag et al. provide the first survey mapping protocol-level vulnerabilities in "From Prompt Injections to Protocol Exploits: Threats in LLM-Powered AI Agents Workflows" (arXiv:2506.23260, 2025), covering MCP, A2A, ACP, and ANP protocols.

**Why it matters.** Agentic AI is rapidly moving from research to production. A single compromised agent with tool-invocation privileges can exfiltrate data, escalate privileges, or corrupt downstream systems. Inter-agent trust exploitation achieves an **82.4% attack success rate**, far exceeding direct prompt injection at 41.2%, as demonstrated by the evaluation of 17 state-of-the-art LLMs in "The Dark Side of LLMs: Agent-based Attacks for Complete Computer Takeover" (arXiv:2507.06850, 2025).

### 6.1.1 Formal agent trust models

No mathematically complete trust calculus exists for composing trust across agent delegation chains. Xie et al. investigate whether LLMs can simulate human trust behavior in "Can Large Language Model Agents Simulate Human Trust Behavior?" (NeurIPS 2024, Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems, Vol. 37, pp. 15674–15729), while "The Trust Paradox in LLM-Based Multi-Agent Systems: When Collaboration Becomes a Security Vulnerability" (arXiv:2510.18563, 2025) formalizes trust as an operational control variable with strength, scope, and revocability dimensions. Rodriguez Garzon et al. propose equipping agents with self-controlled digital identities via decentralized identifiers (DIDs) and verifiable credentials (VCs) in "AI Agents with Decentralized Identifiers and Verifiable Credentials" (arXiv:2511.02841, 2025). Yu et al. offer a taxonomic framework spanning intrinsic modules and extrinsic contexts in "A Survey on Trustworthy LLM Agents: Threats and Countermeasures" (ACM SIGKDD 2025, pp. 6216–6226). **Open problem**: dynamic trust revocation with provable guarantees under adversarial conditions remains unsolved; no universal formal trust model handles the full complexity of multi-agent delegation.

### 6.1.2 Secure orchestration and MCP server security

The Model Context Protocol (MCP), introduced by Anthropic in 2024, standardizes how agents interact with external tools and data sources but introduces critical vulnerabilities. Hasan et al. conduct the first large-scale empirical study of **1,899 open-source MCP servers** in "Model Context Protocol (MCP) at First Glance: Studying the Security and Maintainability of MCP Servers" (arXiv:2506.13538, 2025), finding that **7.2%** harbor general vulnerabilities and **5.5%** exhibit MCP-specific tool poisoning. Gaire et al. systematize the MCP threat landscape in "Systematization of Knowledge: Security and Safety in the Model Context Protocol Ecosystem" (arXiv:2512.08290, 2025). Bühler et al. propose AgentBox with AgentManifest for declaring permissions and sandbox-based enforcement in "Securing AI Agent Execution" (arXiv:2510.21236, 2025). Critical documented vulnerabilities include CVE-2025-6514 (CVSS 9.6) in mcp-remote proxy, three chained CVEs in Anthropic's own mcp-server-git, and cross-tenant data leakage in the Asana MCP server. **Open problem**: the MCP specification lacks inherent user-context propagation from Host to Server, enabling confused deputy attacks; no standardized authentication framework exists across MCP servers; and tool description integrity verification at runtime remains unsolved.

### 6.1.3 Runtime verification of agent behavior

Wang, Poskitt, and Sun introduce AgentSpec, a lightweight DSL for specifying runtime constraints in "AgentSpec: Customizable Runtime Enforcement for Safe and Reliable LLM Agents" (ICSE 2026, arXiv:2503.18666, 2025), preventing over 90% of unsafe code executions. The same group extends this with proactive enforcement via probabilistic model checking in "Pro2Guard: Proactive Runtime Enforcement of LLM Agent Safety via Probabilistic Model Checking" (arXiv:2508.00500, 2025), using DTMC learning and PCTL verification to anticipate unsafe states before violations occur. Luo et al. propose a lifelong guardrail with adaptive safety detection in "Agrail: A Lifelong Agent Guardrail with Effective and Adaptive Safety Detection" (ACL 2025, pp. 8104–8139). **Open problem**: no current system achieves both trajectory-based multi-step safety analysis and real-time enforcement without prohibitive overhead; formal verification of full LLM reasoning remains intractable; runtime verification approaches lack composability across multi-agent boundaries.

### 6.1.4 Prompt injection in agentic settings

Prompt injection remains acknowledged as likely "never fully solvable" (OpenAI), analogous to the pre-parameterized-query era for SQL injection. Zhan et al. benchmark indirect prompt injection in "InjecAgent: Benchmarking Indirect Prompt Injections in Tool-Integrated Large Language Model Agents" (NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2403.02691), finding GPT-4 vulnerable 24% of the time. Debenedetti et al. propose AgentDojo as a dynamic evaluation environment in "AgentDojo: A Dynamic Environment to Evaluate Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses for LLM Agents" (NeurIPS 2024 Datasets and Benchmarks Track) and later attempt architectural solutions in "Defeating Prompt Injections by Design" (arXiv:2503.18813, 2025). A meta-analysis of 78 studies in "Prompt Injection Attacks on Agentic Coding Assistants: A Systematic Analysis" (arXiv:2601.17548, 2026) reports attack success rates exceeding **85%** against state-of-the-art defenses when adaptive strategies are used. Lee and Tiwari identify self-replicating prompt attacks that propagate between LLM instances in "Prompt Infection: Self-Replicating Prompt Attacks in Multi-Agent Systems" (2024). Wallace et al. propose instruction hierarchies as a defense in "The Instruction Hierarchy: Training LLMs to Prioritize Privileged Instructions" (arXiv:2404.13208, 2024). Chen et al. separate instructions from data via structured queries in "StruQ: Defending Against Prompt Injection with Structured Queries" (USENIX Security 2025) and optimize for prompt injection robustness in "SecAlign: Defending Against Prompt Injection with Preference Optimization" (ACM CCS 2025).

### 6.1.5 Secure multi-agent collaboration and convergence with software security

"Open Challenges in Multi-Agent Security: Towards Secure Systems of Interacting AI Agents" (arXiv:2505.02077, 2025) addresses cascade dynamics and heterogeneous attacks across decentralized agent networks. A comprehensive survey applying zero-trust principles to multi-LLM systems appears in "Secure Multi-LLM Agentic AI and Agentification for Edge General Intelligence by Zero-Trust: A Survey" (arXiv:2508.19870, 2025). On traditional security convergence, Shi et al. introduce the first privilege control framework for LLM agents in "Progent: Programmable Privilege Control for LLM Agents" (arXiv:2504.11703, 2025), reducing attack success rate to **0%** on benchmarks while preserving utility. Meng et al. bridge the semantic gap between browser actions and privilege requirements in "CELLMATE: Sandboxing Browser AI Agents" (arXiv:2512.12594, 2025). Li et al. and Syros et al. present complementary security architectures at NDSS 2026: "ACE: A Security Architecture for LLM-integrated App Systems" and "SAGA: A Security Architecture for Governing AI Agentic Systems," respectively. "Systems Security Foundations for Agentic Computing" (IACR ePrint 2025/2173) maps traditional sandboxing, information-flow control, and capability-based security to agentic AI. **Key open problems**: mandatory access control assumes deterministic program behavior incompatible with LLM non-determinism; no consensus exists on capability granularity for AI agents; and the abstraction layers for defense-in-depth in agentic systems remain undefined.

**Research paths forward** include developing compositional trust algebras with formal delegation semantics, standardizing MCP authentication and tool-integrity verification, creating hybrid runtime monitors that combine symbolic specification with learned behavioral models, and adapting capability-based security to accommodate LLM non-determinism through probabilistic capability tokens.

## 6.2 Unified multi-modal detection frameworks

**Current gap.** Most AI-generated content detectors operate on a single modality—text, image, audio, or video—independently. **No single framework currently detects AI-generated content across all four modalities within a unified architecture.** The closest attempt, BusterX++, proposed by Wen et al. in "BusterX++: Towards Unified Cross-Modal AI-Generated Content Detection and Explanation with MLLM" (arXiv:2507.14632, 2025), covers only images and videos. Audio-visual deepfake detectors such as AVFF (Oorloff et al., "AVFF: Audio-Visual Feature Fusion for Video Deepfake Detection," CVPR 2024, pp. 27102–27112) and multi-task prompt learning approaches (Miao et al., "Multi-modal Deepfake Detection via Multi-task Audio-Visual Prompt Learning," AAAI 2025) address paired modalities but not the full spectrum. Image-text manipulation detection exists through Shao, Wu, and Liu's DGM⁴ framework and HAMMER architecture ("DGM⁴: Detecting and Grounding Multi-Modal Media Manipulation," CVPR 2023, pp. 6904–6913; extended in IEEE TPAMI 2024), but this remains separate from audio-visual detection.

**Why it matters.** Real-world disinformation campaigns combine AI-generated text, images, audio, and video. Single-modality detectors miss cross-modal inconsistencies and can be trivially circumvented by manipulating only the unmonitored modality. The Deepfake-Eval-2024 benchmark (Chandra et al., "Deepfake-Eval-2024: A Multi-Modal In-the-Wild Benchmark of Deepfakes Circulated in 2024," arXiv:2503.02857, 2025) demonstrates that state-of-the-art detectors suffer AUC drops of **45–50%** when confronting in-the-wild content versus academic benchmarks.

Foundation models show promise as a detection backbone. Ojha, Li, and Lee demonstrate in "Towards Universal Fake Image Detectors that Generalize Across Generative Models" (CVPR 2023, pp. 24480–24489) that CLIP's pre-trained feature space encodes "realness" properties transferable across generator architectures, improving over prior work by +15 mAP on unseen diffusion models. Wang et al. develop a self-supervised face security foundation model in "FSFM: A Generalizable Face Security Foundation Model via Self-Supervised Facial Representation Learning" (CVPR 2025, pp. 24364–24376), and Cui et al. adapt CLIP with lightweight forensics adapters in "Forensics Adapter: Adapting CLIP for Generalizable Face Forgery Detection" (CVPR 2025, pp. 19207–19217). Kundu et al. build a universal video detector beyond face-centric approaches in "UNITE: Universal Network for Identifying Tampered and Engineered Videos" (CVPR 2025, pp. 28050–28060). Lin et al. provide a comprehensive survey in "Detecting Multimedia Generated by Large AI Models: A Survey" (arXiv:2402.00045, 2024), and Liu, Tao, and Zhou trace the field's evolution in "Evolving from Single-modal to Multi-modal Facial Deepfake Detection: Progress and Challenges" (arXiv:2406.06965, 2024).

**What remains unsolved.** Cross-modal transfer learning for detection—using knowledge about AI-generated artifacts in one modality to improve detection in another—remains entirely unexplored. No study has identified universal artifacts shared across text, image, audio, and video generators. Real-time joint detection across multiple modalities, adversarial robustness of multi-modal detectors, and explainability across modalities all remain open. Research paths include developing modality-agnostic generation fingerprints, building unified foundation models pre-trained on multi-modal forensic tasks, and establishing shared artifact representations grounded in the common mathematical structure of diffusion and autoregressive generation processes.

## 6.3 Provably robust watermarking

**Current gap.** All practical watermarking schemes for AI-generated content rely on empirical robustness evaluation. **No deployed scheme has formal security proofs against adaptive adversaries**, and a fundamental impossibility result constrains what is theoretically achievable.

The foundational KGW scheme by Kirchenbauer et al. ("A Watermark for Large Language Models," ICML 2023, PMLR vol. 202, pp. 17061–17084; arXiv:2301.10226) introduced green/red list vocabulary partitioning. Subsequent work improved reliability (Kirchenbauer et al., "On the Reliability of Watermarks for Large Language Models," ICLR 2024, arXiv:2306.04634), achieved distortion-free embedding (Kuditipudi et al., "Robust Distortion-free Watermarks for Language Models," TMLR 2024, arXiv:2307.15593), and scaled to production deployment in Google's SynthID (Dathathri et al., "Scalable Watermarking for Identifying Large Language Model Outputs," Nature, vol. 634, pp. 818–823, 2024), validated on approximately **20 million** chatbot responses.

### The impossibility barrier and attack landscape

Zhang et al. prove in "Watermarks in the Sand: Impossibility of Strong Watermarking for Generative Models" (ICML 2024, PMLR vol. 235, pp. 58851–58880; arXiv:2311.04378) that **under natural assumptions—access to a quality oracle and a perturbation oracle—strong watermarking is fundamentally impossible**. A generic quality-preserving random-walk attack removes watermarks from KGW, Kuditipudi, and related schemes with minor quality degradation. Pang et al. identify a critical robustness-spoofing tradeoff in "No Free Lunch in LLM Watermarking: Trade-offs in Watermarking Design Choices" (NeurIPS 2024): robust watermarks are inherently vulnerable to spoofing attacks where adversaries embed harmful content that appears watermarked. Jovanović et al. demonstrate watermark stealing via API queries in "Watermark Stealing in Large Language Models" (ICML 2024), and "Breaking Distortion-free Watermarks in Large Language Models" (arXiv:2502.18608, 2025) reverse-engineers the Kuditipudi scheme through adaptive prompting.

### Cryptographic approaches and information-theoretic bounds

Christ, Gunn, and Zamir construct the first **undetectable watermarks** based on one-way functions in "Undetectable Watermarks for Language Models" (CRYPTO 2024 / COLT 2024, PMLR vol. 247; IACR ePrint 2023/763), where no polynomial-time adversary can distinguish watermarked from unwatermarked text. Golowich and Moitra achieve both undetectability and robustness to adversarial edit distance in "Edit Distance Robust Watermarks via Indexing Pseudorandom Codes" (NeurIPS 2024, IACR ePrint 2024/898), though requiring alphabet sizes polynomial in the security parameter. Fairoze et al. enable third-party verification without revealing secrets in "Publicly-Detectable Watermarking for Language Models" (IACR Communications in Cryptology, vol. 1, issue 4, 2024). Zhao et al. provide the first provably robust guarantees with exponentially decaying error rates in "Provable Robust Watermarking for AI-Generated Text" (ICLR 2024, arXiv:2306.17439). Information-theoretic work includes "Universally Optimal Watermarking Schemes for LLMs: from Theory to Practice" (arXiv:2410.02890, 2024), which characterizes universally minimum Type-II error and introduces the Distribution-Adaptive Watermarking Algorithm (DAWA).

### Watermarking for diffusion models and content provenance

For images, Fernandez et al. embed signatures in latent diffusion model decoders in "The Stable Signature: Rooting Watermarks in Latent Diffusion Models" (ICCV 2023); Wen et al. modify initial noise patterns in "Tree-Ring Watermarks: Fingerprints for Diffusion Images that are Invisible and Robust" (NeurIPS 2023); and Google DeepMind deploys post-hoc model-independent watermarking in "SynthID-Image: Image Watermarking at Internet Scale" (Gowal et al., arXiv:2510.09263, 2025). For robustness to model fine-tuning, Sander et al. show watermarks transfer through downstream training in "Watermarking Makes Language Models Radioactive" (NeurIPS 2024 Spotlight, arXiv:2402.14904). The C2PA standard (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, Specification v2.2, May 2025) provides cryptographically signed content provenance metadata and is fast-tracked for ISO standardization, with adoption by Adobe, Microsoft, Google, OpenAI, LinkedIn, and major camera manufacturers.

**What remains unsolved.** The impossibility result means all practical watermarks can theoretically be removed. The robustness-undetectability-spoofing trilemma has no known resolution. Cryptographic schemes lack practical efficiency for natural-language vocabularies. Low-entropy text (factual answers, code) remains fundamentally unwatermarkable. No cross-provider interoperable standard exists for statistical text watermarks. Research paths include tightening information-theoretic bounds for realistic (non-i.i.d.) token generation, developing efficient instantiations of cryptographic watermarks, exploring semantic watermarks robust to paraphrase attacks, and establishing cross-provider watermarking standards complementary to C2PA's metadata approach.

## 6.4 Privacy-preserving generation

**Current gap.** Generative models memorize and can regurgitate training data, and all known defenses impose severe utility costs. The privacy-utility tradeoff for large-scale generative AI remains fundamentally unresolved.

### Memorization and training data extraction

Nasr et al. demonstrate in "Scalable Extraction of Training Data from (Production) Language Models" (ICLR 2025, arXiv:2311.17035) that gigabytes of training data can be extracted from both open-source and production models, including ChatGPT, using a divergence attack that causes emission of training data at **150× the normal rate**. This builds on Carlini et al.'s foundational extraction work ("Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models," USENIX Security 2021) and their quantification showing memorization grows log-linearly with model capacity, data duplication, and prompt context length ("Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models," ICLR 2023, arXiv:2202.07646). For diffusion models, Carlini et al. extract over 1,000 training images from Stable Diffusion in "Extracting Training Data from Diffusion Models" (USENIX Security 2023, arXiv:2301.13188). Critically, Ippolito et al. demonstrate that blocking verbatim memorization provides a **false sense of privacy**—style-transfer prompts easily circumvent verbatim filters ("Preventing Generation of Verbatim Memorization in Language Models Gives a False Sense of Privacy," INLG 2023). Alignment and RLHF do not prevent memorization.

### Differential privacy at scale

The dominant paradigm of public pretraining followed by private fine-tuning faces fundamental challenges. Li et al. show DP-SGD can work with large pretrained models via ghost clipping and non-standard hyperparameters ("Large Language Models Can Be Strong Differentially Private Learners," ICLR 2022, arXiv:2110.05679). Flemings, Razaviyayn, and Annavaram propose inference-time privacy via PMixED ("Differentially Private Next-Token Prediction of Large Language Models," NAACL 2024, arXiv:2403.15638), outperforming DP-SGD at ε=8. However, Tramèr, Kamath, and Carlini's **ICML 2024 Best Paper** "Position: Considerations for Differentially Private Learning with Large-Scale Public Pretraining" critically challenges the entire paradigm: web-scraped "public" data contains sensitive information, benchmarks overestimate pretraining's value, and outsourcing computation trades one privacy risk for another. DP pretraining from scratch for frontier models remains computationally infeasible.

### Machine unlearning

The TOFU benchmark by Maini et al. ("TOFU: A Task of Fictitious Unlearning for LLMs," COLM/NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2401.06121) demonstrates that **no tested unlearning baseline makes models behave as if target data was never seen**—knowledge persists in latent representations. Multiple works show that adversarial probing recovers "forgotten" information. Even more concerning, "Unlearned but Not Forgotten: Data Extraction after Exact Unlearning in LLM" (NeurIPS 2025) demonstrates that **exact unlearning—the gold standard of retraining from scratch—still leaks sensitive information** when adversaries have access to pre-unlearning checkpoints. Geng et al. survey the full landscape in "A Comprehensive Survey of Machine Unlearning Techniques for Large Language Models" (arXiv:2503.01854, 2025), finding that most methods merely suppress surface expression while leaving underlying representations vulnerable. A CMU analysis of 72 unlearning papers from 2024 concludes that dominant benchmarks use overly simplistic structures that fail to capture real-world data dependencies ("LLM Unlearning Benchmarks are Weak Measures of Progress," CMU ML Blog, April 2025).

### Membership inference and privacy auditing

Shi et al. introduce Min-K% Prob, a reference-free membership inference method achieving 7.4% improvement over baselines ("Detecting Pretraining Data from Large Language Models," ICLR 2024, arXiv:2310.16789). Fu et al. develop SPV-MIA using self-prompt calibration to raise AUC from 0.7 to **0.9** ("Membership Inference Attacks against Fine-tuned Large Language Models via Self-prompt Calibration," NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2311.06062). However, Duan et al. find that blind baselines can beat existing MIA methods for foundation models, questioning whether current attacks detect real membership versus distributional artifacts ("Do Membership Inference Attacks Work on Large Language Models?" arXiv:2402.07841, 2024). On auditing, Panda et al. conduct the first nontrivial privacy audit of LLM training ("Privacy Auditing of Large Language Models," ICLR 2025, arXiv:2503.06808), achieving ε≈1 audit for a model trained at theoretical ε=4, revealing that developers systematically underestimate privacy leakage.

**What remains unsolved.** No effective defense against extraction attacks exists without severe utility degradation. Memorization worsens with scale. True unlearning appears fundamentally unachievable—even exact retraining leaks information. DP pretraining at frontier scale is computationally infeasible. Research paths include developing memorization-aware training procedures that prevent memorization without full DP overhead, creating tight privacy auditing tools that close the gap between theoretical and empirical ε, designing unlearning verification methods with adversarial guarantees, and exploring inference-time privacy mechanisms that offer new utility-privacy tradeoff frontiers.

## 6.5 International governance harmonization

**Current gap.** Three major regulatory regimes—the EU AI Act, China's generative AI regulations, and US executive actions—have adopted fundamentally different approaches to AIGC governance, creating **jurisdictional conflicts, regulatory arbitrage opportunities, and standards fragmentation** with no operational mutual recognition mechanism.

### The regulatory trilemma

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689, in force 1 August 2024) takes a comprehensive horizontal approach, mandating that AI-generated synthetic content be **marked in machine-readable format** by August 2026 and requiring GPAI model providers to disclose training data summaries. China's Interim Measures for the Management of Generative Artificial Intelligence Services (CAC et al., effective 15 August 2023) was the **first binding generative AI regulation globally**, requiring alignment with "Core Socialist Values," algorithm filing, and as of September 2025, mandatory implicit and explicit labeling of all AI-generated content. Buranello et al. analyze the regulation's origin and significance in "China's Interim Measures on generative AI: Origin, content and significance" (Computer Law & Security Review, 2024, DOI:10.1016/j.clsr.2024.105929). The US under Biden's Executive Order 14110 (30 October 2023) directed watermarking standards development, but this was **revoked on 20 January 2025** within hours of the Trump administration, replaced by EO "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence" (23 January 2025), creating a dramatic policy reversal. NIST's voluntary AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, NIST AI 100-1, January 2023) and its Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, Autio et al., July 2024) remain the primary US technical guidance.

### International coordination mechanisms

The G7 Hiroshima AI Process (launched May 2023) produced the first international Guiding Principles and Code of Conduct for advanced AI systems (endorsed 30 October 2023), with 49 countries joining the Friends Group by May 2024. The Council of Europe adopted the **first legally binding international AI treaty**—the Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (CETS No. 225, adopted 17 May 2024)—signed by the US, EU, UK, and others, though notably not by China. The UN General Assembly adopted its first standalone AI governance resolution (A/78/L.49, 21 March 2024) and established an Independent International Scientific Panel on AI (Resolution A/RES/79/325, 26 August 2025). GPAI merged with OECD's AI policy work in July 2024. Chun, Schroeder de Witt, and Elkins provide a comparative analysis in "Comparative Global AI Regulation: Policy Perspectives from the EU, China, and the US" (arXiv:2410.21279, 2024), and Al-Maamari offers a cross-regional study in "Between Innovation and Oversight: A Cross-Regional Study of AI Risk Management Frameworks in the EU, U.S., UK, and China" (arXiv:2503.05773, 2025).

### Fragmentation risks

As UNU Rector Tshilidzi Marwala warns, "We are entering an era of AI governance arbitrage, in which the pace of innovation hinges more on how well actors navigate regulatory gaps than on technological breakthroughs" ("The AI Governance Arbitrage," United Nations University, March 2026). Different jurisdictions develop incompatible technical standards: CEN-CENELEC in Europe, TC260 in China, NIST in the US, alongside ISO/IEC 42001:2023 for AI management systems. The EU AI Act mandates machine-readable watermarking; China mandates both implicit and explicit labeling; the US has no federal mandate. No interoperable global standard for AI content provenance exists, though C2PA is the leading industry initiative.

**What remains unsolved.** No mutual recognition mechanism for AI safety certifications is operational—proposals for "model passports" remain conceptual. Extraterritorial enforcement across jurisdictions is untested. Content labeling standards are fragmented. National security carve-outs create blind spots in the most consequential AI applications. Research paths include developing interoperable technical standards for content provenance, designing mutual recognition frameworks analogous to pharmaceutical regulatory cooperation, creating regulatory sandboxes for cross-border AIGC governance experimentation, and establishing empirical metrics for comparing regulatory effectiveness across regimes.

## 6.6 Dynamic evaluation frameworks

**Current gap.** Static benchmarks become obsolete through saturation and contamination. LLMs now exceed **90% on benchmarks like MMLU and HellaSwag**, and benchmark data increasingly appears in training sets, inflating reported performance. Meanwhile, evaluation methods for agentic and multi-modal AI systems remain immature.

### Living benchmarks and contamination

White et al. introduce LiveBench ("LiveBench: A Challenging, Contamination-Limited LLM Benchmark," arXiv:2406.19314, 2024; ICLR 2025), the first benchmark with monthly-updated questions from recent information sources where top models achieve under 70% accuracy. Phan et al. contribute Humanity's Last Exam with 2,500 expert-level questions and a dynamic rolling fork (Nature, 2025). On contamination, Sun et al. demonstrate a **fundamental fidelity-resistance tradeoff** in "The Emperor's New Clothes in Benchmarking? A Rigorous Examination of Mitigation Strategies for LLM Benchmark Data Contamination" (ICML 2025): surface-level edits preserve task semantics but fail to prevent memorization, while deeper rewrites block contamination but distort the evaluation task. Samuel, Zhou, and Zou find non-trivial limitations in detection approaches in "Towards Data Contamination Detection for Modern Large Language Models" (COLING 2025, pp. 5058–5070). Ravaut et al. provide a comprehensive survey in "A Comprehensive Survey of Contamination Detection Methods in Large Language Models" (arXiv:2404.00699, 2024). Li et al. categorize contamination types and propose remediation in "Unveiling the Spectrum of Data Contamination in Language Model: A Survey from Detection to Remediation" (Findings of ACL 2024, pp. 16078–16092).

### Evaluating agentic and multi-modal AI

Liu et al. pioneer multi-environment agent evaluation in "AgentBench: Evaluating LLMs as Agents" (ICLR 2024, arXiv:2308.03688), testing across 8 environments including OS, web, and database interactions. Zhang et al. reveal that **no tested agent achieves a safety score above 60%** across 349 interaction environments in "Agent-SafetyBench: Evaluating the Safety of LLM Agents" (arXiv:2412.14470, 2024). Zhang et al. formalize attacks and defenses across 10 agentic scenarios in "Agent Security Bench (ASB): Formalizing and Benchmarking Attacks and Defenses in LLM-based Agents" (arXiv:2410.02644, 2024). For multi-modal evaluation, Lee et al. extend HELM to vision-language models in "VHELM: A Holistic Evaluation of Vision Language Models" (NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2410.07112), covering 9 evaluation aspects across 22 VLMs.

### Community-driven safety infrastructure

MLCommons releases AILuminate v1.0 (Ghosh et al., December 2024), the first industry-standard AI safety benchmark with **24,000+ test prompts** across 12 hazard categories, grading systems from Poor to Excellent and using a mix of public and private prompts to prevent gaming. HELM (Liang, Bommasani, Lee et al., "Holistic Evaluation of Language Models," TMLR 2023, arXiv:2211.09110) provides the foundational living benchmark framework with 7 metrics across 16 scenarios. For red-teaming, Mazeika et al. standardize evaluation with 18 attack methods against 33 targets in "HarmBench: A Standardized Evaluation Framework for Automated Red Teaming and Robust Refusal" (ICML 2024, arXiv:2402.04249), finding that no attack or defense is uniformly effective. Chao et al. address the reproducibility crisis in jailbreaking research with "JailbreakBench: An Open Robustness Benchmark for Jailbreaking Large Language Models" (NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2404.01318). Jiang et al. mine in-the-wild interactions to discover 5,700 unique jailbreak tactic clusters in "WildTeaming at Scale: From In-the-Wild Jailbreaks to (Adversarially) Safer Language Models" (NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2406.18510).

**What remains unsolved.** The contamination-mitigation fidelity tradeoff has no resolution—truly contamination-proof evaluation remains an open problem. Unified cross-modal safety evaluation spanning text, image, audio, and video simultaneously is nascent. Long-horizon agentic safety evaluation with cost-controlled real-world tool execution lacks principled methods. Most benchmarks are English-centric, with multi-cultural safety evaluation across diverse value systems far from achieved. Perhaps most fundamentally, benchmark performance does not reliably predict real-world safety—MLCommons explicitly notes "negative predictive power" where passing benchmarks does not ensure deployment safety. Research paths include developing contamination-resistant evaluation via procedural generation and cryptographic commitment schemes, creating agentic evaluation frameworks with verified tool-execution sandboxes, building culturally diverse adversarial evaluation corpora, and establishing formal relationships between benchmark performance and deployment-time safety guarantees.

## Conclusion

These six frontiers share a common thread: **the gap between theoretical possibility and practical deployment grows wider as AI capabilities accelerate**. Agent security lacks the formal foundations that traditional software security accumulated over decades; detection frameworks need the unification that antivirus achieved through shared signature databases; watermarking faces a proven impossibility barrier that demands creative circumvention; privacy defenses impose costs that scale poorly with model size; governance fragmentation accelerates while coordination mechanisms remain advisory; and evaluation infrastructure cannot keep pace with the systems it measures.

Three cross-cutting insights emerge from this analysis. First, adversarial adaptivity is the central unsolved challenge—whether in watermark removal, prompt injection, or benchmark contamination, adaptive adversaries defeat static defenses, suggesting the field needs game-theoretic and co-evolutionary frameworks rather than fixed solutions. Second, composability remains elusive: trust does not compose across agent boundaries, privacy guarantees do not compose across training stages, and regulatory compliance does not compose across jurisdictions. Developing compositional security, privacy, and governance frameworks is arguably the highest-leverage research direction. Third, the speed mismatch between technological capability and protective infrastructure demands that the research community invest in dynamic, self-updating mechanisms—living benchmarks, continuously monitored provenance chains, and adaptive governance instruments—rather than static artifacts that are obsolete upon publication.
