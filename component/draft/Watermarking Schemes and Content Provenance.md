# [C13] §5.2 Watermarking Schemes and Content Provenance

## Text Watermarking

**KGW scheme (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023):**  The original LLM watermark (often called “KGW”) works by partitioning the vocabulary into a **green list** and **red list** based on a pseudorandom generator seeded by the *preceding* token(s).  Concretely, each time the model generates a token, a hash of the last token (or context) seeds an RNG to split the vocabulary (typically 50–50) into “green” vs. “red” words【6†L13-L21】.  During generation the model’s logits are biased in favor of green words (for example by adding a positive constant *w* to green tokens’ logits)【6†L19-L23】.  In the extreme “hard” variant, red tokens are forbidden entirely; in the soft variant only their probability is reduced.  This ensures that a watermarked model tends to produce more green words.  For detection, one simply counts green vs. red tokens in a suspect text and performs a statistical **z-test** against the expected 50% rate【6†L24-L28】.  A high z-score (above some threshold *c*, e.g. 2.5 or 4) indicates a watermark.  KGW’s strengths are its simplicity and provable detection: no model retraining is required (only modifying the sampling step) and one obtains a statistically sound test of “AI-generated” vs. natural text.  Its weaknesses include generation quality degradation (larger bias *w* makes text slightly less natural) and poor robustness: even light editing (e.g. paraphrasing or replacing words) quickly destroys the green-word signal.  Indeed, recent work shows adversarial paraphrase attacks can almost entirely remove the watermark【8†L49-L57】.

**Unigram watermark (Zhao et al., 2023):**  This variant simplifies KGW by using a fixed partition of the vocabulary rather than a dynamic one.  In Unigram-Watermark, the entire vocab is split once (seeded by a secret key) into green and red sets, independent of context【15†L289-L297】.  Generation is biased by adding δ to green logits.  Because the partition is fixed, each token carries an independent watermark bit, which makes the scheme more robust to re-orderings and to moderate edits.  Zhao *et al.* prove that with a suitably chosen δ one can tolerate limited synonym substitutions or deletions.  Detection is the same green-count z-test as KGW.  The trade-off is that a fixed partition can in principle be learned by an adversary given many samples (“learnability”), and the watermark can still degrade quality if δ is large【15†L321-L327】【15†L336-L342】.  However, empirically Unigram-watermark degrades text quality less than KGW for comparable detection rates and survives moderate paraphrasing【15†L321-L327】.

**Multi-bit watermarking:**  Instead of a single binary bit (watermarked or not), recent schemes embed multi-bit messages (e.g. user IDs or timestamps) into text.  For example, Qu *et al.* (2024) design a watermark that encodes a 20–32 bit identifier by pseudo-randomly assigning each token position to a segment of bits and using error-correcting (Reed–Solomon) codes【75†L37-L45】.  During generation they bias tokens so that each segment of text statistically encodes the target bits.  In extraction they recover the noisy codeword from the text and decode the Reed–Solomon code.  They report robust extraction of 20–32 bit messages with moderate edits (tolerance ~17 token substitutions on average)【75†L37-L45】.  Other works similarly enumerate messages or assign bit patterns to tokens (e.g. Yoo *et al.*, Boroujeny *et al.*)【74†L79-L87】.  Multi-bit watermarks support traceability of content to a source ID, but require more complex encoding/decoding and are generally more sensitive to edits. 

**SemStamp (Hou et al., 2023):**  SemStamp proposes a *sentence-level* watermarking: each generated sentence is hashed into a semantic embedding space and the model is forced (via rejection sampling) to only accept sentences whose embedding falls into a predetermined target region【20†L53-L61】.  In practice, after generating each sentence, SemStamp checks an LSH-hash of its sentence embedding against a stored “watermarked” set; if it misses, the sentence is regenerated.  This embeds a high-level watermark in meaning rather than individual tokens.  Hou *et al.* show this scheme is much more robust to paraphrase attacks (synonym substitutions, rewriting) than token-based schemes, while still producing fluent text【20†L53-L61】.  The trade-off is sampling overhead (many rejections) and complexity of embedding hashing. 

**Distortion-free (“undetectable”) watermarks (Christ et al., 2024; Kuditipudi et al., 2023):**  A new line of work asks: can we watermark without any change to the output distribution (i.e. no quality loss)?  Christ *et al.* (2024) formalize this idea: they design cryptographic sampling procedures (e.g. inverse-transform sampling with a secret key) so that the **watermarked model’s output distribution is *identical*** to the original.  In such a scheme, without the secret key no statistical test can distinguish watermarked from unwatermarked text【22†L17-L24】.  They show that this “undetectable” property is achievable under one-way function assumptions, effectively shifting the watermark to a secret-key signature.  Kuditipudi *et al.* (2023) propose a practical version: during sampling they generate an extra pseudo-random noise and use it to pick tokens via *inverse sampling* or *minimum-entropy sampling*.  The result is provably zero distributional distortion but a detectable watermark for key-holders.  These schemes have the advantage of **no degradation of text quality** (imperceptible to language metrics) and very high robustness to moderate edits (detection survives even 40–50% token corruption)【34†L53-L61】.  The downside is complexity: they require special sampling code (no plain softmax) and rely on secret keys, and they may still fail under heavy paraphrase (if the adversary knows the method, they could perform an appropriate inverse sampling with wrong key).  

**DiPmark (Wu et al., 2023):**  This is a distribution-preserving watermark.  DiPmark pre-selects a random subset of token indices as “watermark candidates” and then applies a reweighting function during sampling that biases those tokens in a way that precisely preserves the overall distribution【28†L63-L72】.  In effect, DiPmark hides the signal in higher-order token correlations while leaving unigram frequencies unchanged.  It is “stealthy” (no visible distribution shift) and robust by design, and detection is done via the secret seed.  The authors prove DiPmark is resilient to moderate corruption.  In practice, it avoids the quality losses of KGW/Unigram at the expense of computational overhead.  

**KTH scheme (Kuditipudi et al., 2023):**  Along the same line, Kuditipudi, Thickstun, and Hashimoto (KTH) proposed a *robust distortion-free* watermark that uses a keyed random seed in the sampling process.  Their method injects randomness into the sampling (similar to DiPmark) so that the output distribution is unchanged.  They demonstrate detection can survive even ~40% token edits【34†L53-L61】.  Essentially, KTH’s approach and DiPmark show that with cryptographic keys one can get provable imperceptibility, again shifting the watermark from model weights to sampling randomness.  

**Post-hoc text watermarking:**  All the above require control of the generation process.  Post-hoc methods try to “add” a watermark after the fact.  Early approaches (hand-crafted steganography) simply replace words with synonyms or alter punctuation to encode bits, but these have very low capacity and break easily【36†L97-L105】.  Some neural steganography models have been proposed (e.g. encoding one hidden sentence in another), but they tend to be brittle.  A promising recent idea is to use an LLM: feed the original text to a watermarked LLM and let it paraphrase or continue in a way that imprints the watermark【36†L73-L82】.  Fernandez *et al.* (2025) evaluate exactly this: they run a watermarked model (using various schemes) on an existing text as a “prefix” and let it rewrite the content.  They find it can work well for narrative text (books, stories) with careful decoding (e.g. Gumbel-max sampling)【36†L73-L82】, but it is still problematic for highly structured content (e.g. source code) where preserving semantics is hard【36†L153-L161】.  In summary, post-hoc watermarking trades off fidelity: it can protect authored text by re-encoding it, but it cannot guarantee the same style/meaning. 

**Quality–Strength–Robustness Trade-off:**  As in all watermarking, there is a three-way tension.  A stronger watermark (easier to detect, more bits embedded) typically causes more distortion to the model’s output distribution, harming quality.  A subtler watermark preserves quality but may be fragile (e.g. removed by small edits).  Christ *et al.* formalize this: an undetectable watermark implies an unaltered distribution【22†L17-L24】, meaning no statistical clues unless one has a secret key.  In practice, most schemes must pick a bias strength (δ or *w*) that balances detection power vs. perplexity.  WaterMax (Giboulot & Furon, 2024) even claims to *break* the usual detectability–quality trade-off by a clever optimal biasing, but in general one accepts that *E[I(W;text)]* must increase with distortion.  In short: one can have at most two of (high fidelity, high bit-rate, high edit robustness) without loss in the third. 

**Attacks on text watermarks:**  Several attacks have been demonstrated.  The most potent is **paraphrasing**: targeted rewriting using LLMs can almost fully erase simple watermarks.  Cheng *et al.* (2025) show that a self-supervised paraphraser (SIRA) can remove KGW or Unigram watermarks with ~100% success at negligible quality cost【8†L49-L57】.  Other simple transformations also work: machine translation and back-translation tend to scramble token lists, truncating the text (short excerpts) can lose the signal, and mixing watermarked text with human-written text dilutes the green count.  A different threat is **spoofing**: forging or inducing a watermark signal in human text.  For example, Sadasivan *et al.* (2023) describe a “forgery” attack that analyzes green-word frequencies and then inserts tokens to mimic a watermark in arbitrary text.  Pang *et al.* (2024) demonstrate a “piggyback” attack: drastically changing the meaning of a passage (e.g. injecting hateful content) while preserving the original watermark bits, misleading attributions【41†L91-L100】.  In short, an adversary can either try to remove a watermark (paraphrase, style-transfer, generative rewrite) or to inject a false watermark signal.  

**Recent deployments (2024–2025):**  In industry, Google DeepMind has begun deploying text watermarking.  In May 2024 they announced **SynthID for text**: an open-source toolkit that subtly adjusts token probabilities during generation (similar in spirit to KGW) to tag model output【45†L221-L230】.  Google reports SynthID-text works best on longer, creative responses and is robust to cropping or mild edits, but heavy rewriting or translation can break it【45†L249-L254】.  Google aims to integrate this into their Gemini (Bard) service by late 2024【45†L201-L205】.  (By contrast, other major models are quieter: OpenAI and Meta have not publicly detailed their watermarking for text as of 2025.)  These efforts show movement toward real-world watermarking, but standardization is lacking: different vendors use different schemes. 

## Image Watermarking

**Generation-time (diffusion-native) watermarking:**  These techniques embed watermarks during the generative process, often in the diffusion latents or decoder.

- **Stable Signature (Fernandez et al., 2023):**  This method fine-tunes a pre-trained latent diffusion model’s decoder to emit a hidden signature in every image【50†L9-L17】【50†L79-L88】.  Specifically, one conditions on a binary signature and trains the decoder (with a perceptual loss and an auxiliary extractor network) so that the output image contains an invisible watermark.  At detection time a learned extractor recovers the signature bits from a generated image, and a statistical test verifies them【50†L9-L17】.  Because the watermark is built into the model weights, it appears in *all* outputs.  The authors report high accuracy: e.g. even if only 10% of the image remains after an extreme crop, the detector still identifies the model origin with >90% accuracy (false-alarm <10^−6)【50†L18-L24】.  The advantage is strong robustness (cropping, resizing, etc.) with no post-hoc processing.  The downsides are that it requires access to and modification of the generator network (fine-tuning) and that if the model is made open, the watermark can in principle be removed by re-training the decoder【50†L71-L80】.

- **Tree-Ring Watermarks (Wen et al., 2023):**  This method embeds a “fingerprint” directly into the *initial noise* of the diffusion process【52†L53-L61】.  A special pattern is added to the noise in the frequency domain (a concentric ring pattern) before any diffusion steps.  This pattern is chosen so that it survives convolutions, crops, rotations, etc.【52†L55-L63】.  After an image is generated, one “inverts” the diffusion (maps image back to noise) and checks for the fingerprint in the recovered noise.  Wen *et al.* show this works on arbitrary diffusion models (text- or image-conditioned) with negligible loss in image quality.  Because the watermark is carried in the random seed, it is invisible and highly robust: typical image edits (JPEG compression, geometric transforms) do not remove the Fourier-pattern fingerprint.  This method has been reported to be much more robust than simple post-hoc watermarks. 

- **Gaussian Shading (Yang et al., 2024):**  This scheme embeds the watermark by slightly perturbing the diffusion latent in a way that preserves its Gaussian distribution【54†L57-L66】.  Rather than fine-tune the model, it modifies the stochastic sampling: it maps the watermark message onto a shift in the latent variables whose overall effect is “statistically invisible” (the latents still look like i.i.d. Gaussian)【54†L60-L68】.  In practice, the method is “plug-and-play” and does not change model weights【54†L57-L64】.  They prove the watermarking is performance-lossless (no change in image metrics) and show high robustness: even after common distortions (e.g. JPEG, cropping) the watermark can be extracted reliably【54†L66-L71】.  The watermark is recovered by inverting the diffusion (e.g. via DDIM inversion) and reading off the encoded bits.

**Post-generation (encoder–decoder) watermarking:**  These apply a separate neural network to embed a watermark *after* the image is generated (or on any image).  Classic examples include:

- **HiDDeN (Zhu et al., 2018):**  A CNN-based approach where an encoder network takes an image and a bitstring and outputs a watermarked image, and a paired decoder network extracts the bits.  The network is trained end-to-end with simulated distortions (JPEG compression, cropping, noise) between encoder and decoder, to force robustness【57†L129-L137】.  In HiDDeN, a noise layer is inserted during training to represent likely corruptions (e.g. JPEG, scaling)【57†L129-L137】.  The result is an invisible watermark that survives moderate image manipulations. 

- **StegaStamp (Tancik et al., 2020):**  An improved end-to-end steganography for images.  Like HiDDeN, StegaStamp uses an encoder CNN and decoder CNN to hide bits (e.g. a 100-bit hyperlink) in an image, but with an emphasis on real-world robustness【57†L98-L106】.  In training, StegaStamp simulates printing and camera capture (differentiable warping, perspective, blur, noise) so that the watermark survives physical-world distortions【57†L98-L106】.  In experiments, StegaStamp recovers ~95% of bits even after a printed photo is taken and JPEG compressed【57†L98-L106】.  Compared to traditional watermarks (DCT/SVD-based), these learned methods can adapt to complex corruptions but require retraining for new conditions. 

These DNN-based watermarks differ from classical ones in that they learn an embedding that is often more tolerant of various distortions, at the cost of training complexity.  In practice they achieve very high capacity (tens to hundreds of bits) and invisibility, but can still be broken by extreme edits.

**Generation-time vs. Post-generation:**  Generation-time schemes (Stable Signature, Tree-Ring, Gaussian Shading, SynthID) integrate with the model so that watermarking is intrinsic to every output image.  They tend to be very robust to transformations (since the watermark lives in the latent/code) and impose no extra post-processing.  However, they require control of the model internals.  Post-generation methods (HiDDeN, StegaStamp, SynthID-Image’s external model) can be applied to any image, but must explicitly simulate robustness (JPEG, crop, affine, etc.) in training.  In general, generation-time watermarks can survive extreme operations (e.g. aggressive cropping) that might wipe out a post-hoc watermark, whereas post-hoc watermarks are more flexible (work on arbitrary images and even real photos) but often have narrower robustness specifications.  

**Robustness evaluation:**  Watermark papers typically test common transformations.  For example, Fernandez *et al.* report that Stable Signature can still identify an image as generated even if 90% of its pixels are cropped away【50†L18-L24】.  StegaStamp achieves ~95% bit-recovery after full JPEG compression (quality 50–100) and even under perspective warp and moderate blur【57†L98-L106】.  Yang *et al.* (Gaussian Shading) claim full recovery after typical lossy editing (JPEG, color jitter)【54†L66-L70】.  More systematically, SynthID-Image’s deployment paper evaluates dozens of transformations (resize, brightness/contrast, blur, JPEG, additive noise) and reports >99% detection accuracy under most common edits (see Gowal *et al.* for details).  One notable class of attacks is **regeneration:** using an uncontrolled diffusion model to “wash” the image.  Zhao *et al.* (2023) show that adding noise to the latent and resampling (the “regeneration” attack) can remove weak, low-distortion watermarks while keeping the image looking similar【59†L91-L100】.  Strong watermarks (high distortion) like Tree-Ring require heavy noising to erase, which then visibly degrades the image【59†L99-L108】.  

**Google’s SynthID (images):**  Independently, Google/DeepMind have developed **SynthID-Image** (2023–24), used on DALL·E 3.  SynthID-Image is a deployed DNN-based watermark: Gowal *et al.* (2024) report it has been used to watermark *over ten billion* images on Google services【77†L79-L87】.  It is a generation-aware watermark (based on diffusion layers) that meets stringent invisibility and robustness requirements.  In evaluations, SynthID-Image outperforms prior methods on both image quality and resistance to JPEG, resize, crop, etc.  (The full details are in Gowal *et al.* 2025【77†L79-L87】.)  Google has made SynthID-Image’s verification service available to partners, and an open variant (**SynthID-O**) is being used externally.  The general trend is that internet-scale watermarking is maturing: deployed systems combine deep learning, noise injection, and careful threat modeling to achieve high detection rates under real-world conditions【77†L79-L87】.

## Content Provenance Systems

**C2PA / Content Credentials:**  The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) is a cross-industry standard (backed by Adobe, Microsoft, Google, Sony, Nikon, Canon, Leica, etc.【66†L100-L104】) for carrying *signed metadata* (“Content Credentials”) in media files.  Under C2PA, every asset (image, video, audio) can carry a cryptographically signed manifest recording its provenance.  At a technical level, a manifest is a JSON bundle of assertions (e.g. “captured by X camera at time T”, “edited with tool Y at version Z”, “AI tools used: yes/no”) that is hashed and signed by the issuing device or software【63†L173-L184】【66†L114-L123】.  The signature ties the metadata to the file (via a content hash), so any tampering of the history breaks the signature.  

A typical **workflow** is as follows (cf. Dewis, 2026【66†L114-L123】):  
1. **Capture:** A C2PA-enabled device (say, a camera or smartphone) creates an initial manifest at shot time.  It records the device ID, time, location, and a hash of the image, and signs this manifest with the manufacturer’s key.  
2. **Edit:** When the image is opened in an editing tool (e.g. Adobe Photoshop/Lightroom, Microsoft Clipchamp), the tool appends new assertions (“applied filter X”, “used generative fill”) to a copy of the manifest, re-hashing the image and edits, and re-signs it with the user or software’s key.  Each step in the pipeline adds a layer to the trust chain.  
3. **Export:** Upon export/publishing, the user chooses to embed the Content Credentials into the file (e.g. in a binary chunk or metadata) and/or publish it to a cloud storage.  The C2PA manifest travels with the file.  
4. **Verify:** A verifier (e.g. a website plugin, mobile app, or cloud service) can read the embedded manifest, verify each signature against a known root of trust, and display the provenance chain. If anything (pixels or metadata) has been altered illegitimately, the signatures will not check out.  

In practice, tools and devices have begun to implement C2PA: as of 2026 the Google Pixel 10 smartphone natively supports C2PA signing in its camera app【79†L151-L159】, and Fujifilm has rolled it out to cameras like the X-T50 and GFX100S II【79†L157-L160】.  Adobe’s Lightroom and Photoshop have “Content Credentials” export options so that post-processing steps (including use of generative AI features) are recorded【79†L164-L173】.  On the platform side, LinkedIn will display a provenance badge for images carrying C2PA data, and Google’s “Trusted Images” on Android uses C2PA (plus SynthID) to flag authentic vs. AI-generated content【79†L249-L256】.  The ecosystem around C2PA – now called Content Credentials – is large: by end of 2025 the Content Authenticity Initiative (the coalition behind C2PA) had over 6000 members across media, tech, and journalism【66†L100-L104】【79†L258-L263】. 

**Limitations of C2PA:**  The main weakness is that the metadata can be stripped or never applied.  Many social media platforms (e.g. Facebook, Instagram) currently remove all metadata on upload, which strips out any embedded C2PA manifest【79†L269-L277】.  Even with devices and tools supporting C2PA, there is a “chicken-and-egg” problem: photographers and camera-makers hesitate to sign images if no one will verify, and platforms wait for a critical mass of signed content【79†L278-L287】.  Early surveys found almost no images online carried C2PA tags as of 2025.  Moreover, C2PA itself only attests to *signed history*; it does not prove “truth” – it cannot prevent a malicious user from lying in the manifest, only detect if the manifest has been tampered with【63†L115-L124】.  Finally, if metadata is stripped, one cannot verify provenance; cloud backup of credentials partially addresses this, but it requires extra infrastructure.

**Blockchain-based approaches:**  An alternative provenance idea is to anchor content hashes on a blockchain.  Several proposals suggest hashing content (or its fingerprint) and writing that hash into a distributed ledger as an immutable timestamp.  The advantage is decentralization and permanence: for example, one could anchor a merkle root of millions of asset hashes in one blockchain transaction【71†L232-L241】【71†L247-L256】.  This gives a public tamper-proof log of “file X existed on date T”.  InCyan’s whitepaper (2024) describes a “hash-to-chain” model: content identifiers are batch-added to a blockchain, with Merkle proofs for verification【71†L232-L241】【71†L263-L272】.  When combined with watermarking, the idea is that the watermark carries a content ID which is then resolved to the blockchain record.  The downside of pure blockchain approaches is cost and granularity: you typically store only hashes on-chain (not actual data), and you need off-chain systems to manage metadata, licensing, etc.【71†L247-L256】.  Also, blockchains give a timestamp but not an easy way to tie together transformed derivatives (a cropped image won’t match the original hash).  In practice, blockchain anchoring is still largely experimental; it promises immutable attestation, but it is not yet widely adopted for routine content provenance (unlike C2PA). 

**Content Credentials ecosystem:**  The broader infrastructure includes verification tools and registries.  For example, Adobe provides a *Content Credentials Cloud* where users can upload manifests【79†L269-L277】, and browser plugins or apps (e.g. contentcredentials.org/verify) allow anyone to check an image’s provenance chain【66†L114-L123】.  In addition to metadata, there are efforts to combine these credentials with invisible watermarks or perceptual hashes.  One idea is that a watermark (like SynthID) can carry a key or ID that references a blockchain or C2PA record, creating a unified “fingerprint + proof” chain【71†L315-L324】.  The CAI (now C2PA) community also fosters standards (IPTC for publishing, W3C for web galleries, etc.).  In short, Content Credentials are one pillar of an emerging provenance infrastructure that includes devices, software, cloud services, and (potentially) blockchain logs. 

## Challenges and Limitations

**Robustness vs. Imperceptibility:**  Fundamental limits exist.  Any detectable watermark necessarily alters the content distribution to some extent.  Christ *et al.* prove that a perfectly undetectable watermark (no distribution change) requires cryptographic secrecy【22†L17-L24】.  In practice, making a watermark more robust (stronger signal) will often make it more perceptible or easier to detect by an attacker.  SynthID-Image’s developers explicitly note that “achieving robustness to a large range of different threat models has been shown to be particularly tricky; this will either reduce performance or require a more visible watermark”【81†L55-L64】.  Thus, there is likely an information-theoretic trade-off: increasing one of (fidelity, capacity, robustness) reduces the others.  Watermarks like DiPmark and distortion-free schemes try to push the boundary (minimal distortion), but they rely on secret keys or complex schemes – and if an attacker reverse-engineers them, the watermark can become effectively invisible.

**Watermark removal by model editing:**  Open-source model watermarks can often be erased by retraining or fine-tuning.  For example, Xu *et al.* (2025) show that if one merges a watermarked Llama model with the unwatermarked original at a 50:50 ratio, the watermark’s detection AUC drops to ~0.5 (i.e. undetectable)【32†L169-L177】.  In general, any fine-tuning on new data or model distillation can reduce or destroy the subtle biases that encode the watermark.  This is especially true for model-level watermarks (where the weight updates encode the watermark): if the model is public, an adversary can retrain it away.  

**Lack of standardization:**  Today each provider or research group has its own scheme.  Google’s SynthID-Text/text and SynthID-Image, OpenAI’s internal methods (undisclosed), Meta’s experiments – none are interoperable.  There is no single “format” for watermarks in text or images.  This fragmentation means verifiers must implement many detectors, and attackers need only target one scheme to “break” the ecosystem.  (Note: this is in contrast to Content Credentials, which *are* standardized by C2PA.)

**Voluntary vs. mandated compliance:**  Currently watermarking/provenance is voluntary.  Some governments (e.g. EU AI Act) are discussing requiring AI-generated content to be labeled or watermarked, but no enforcement exists yet.  Without legal mandates, adoption relies on company policies and user incentives.  For example, photo competitions may *encourage* C2PA metadata, but not all participants comply【79†L238-L247】.  If watermarking remains voluntary, some content will continue to circulate unmarked (which undermines the goal of traceability).  

**Adversarial robustness:**  Even beyond paraphrasing, more powerful attackers could use adversarial ML to remove watermarks.  For images, “re-generation” attacks (using another diffusion or GAN model to recreate the image) can erase invisible watermarks while keeping visuals close【59†L91-L100】.  For text, one could train a “denoiser” model that projects any text back to high-probability sequences under the clean model, thereby eliminating the green-word bias.  SynthID-Image’s threat analysis warns that white-box attacks (where the adversary knows the watermarking key or model) can trivially break any secret-key scheme.  In short, no watermark is perfectly secure against a dedicated adversary.  

**Verification infrastructure:**  Finally, practical questions remain: who runs the verifiers?  Watermark detectors typically require secret keys or models to verify.  C2PA verification needs trust in certificate authorities.  Without a unified authority, users must rely on third-party tools.  Social platforms could automatically check for watermarks/credentials, but if they choose not to, content will go unchecked.  Content credentials do not solve “who should check”: they only provide the data if someone cares to look.  Until there is widespread, user-friendly tooling (or legal requirements) to check watermarks and provenance, their mere existence has limited effect.  

**In summary,** while many effective text and image watermarking schemes and provenance standards have been proposed and even deployed, each comes with trade-offs. Watermarks can be made strong or stealthy but not both; they can be statistically detectable but then fragile. Provenance via metadata is robust but easily removed by platform pipelines. Ongoing research continues to push these frontiers (e.g. information-theoretic undetectable watermarks, hybrid blockchain+watermark systems), but fundamental limits and deployment challenges mean that no silver bullet exists. Any solution will likely need a combination of techniques (metadata, watermarks, and forensic analysis) along with policy and ecosystem support to be reliable in practice.
