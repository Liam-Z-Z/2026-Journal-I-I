# [C02] §2.4 From Content Generation to Autonomous Action: The Agentic Capability Shift

## 2.4.0 Overview

The preceding sections examined AI systems as producers of static content—text, images, audio, and video—where the security concern centered on what a model *outputs*. This section marks a fundamental pivot in the threat landscape. A convergence of four technical advances—reasoning-capable models, agentic architectures with tool invocation, million-token context windows, and standardized tool integration protocols—has transformed large language models from passive generators into **autonomous agents capable of executing multi-step plans in real computing environments**. The security problem is no longer "AI generates harmful content" but rather "AI autonomously executes harmful actions," a qualitative shift that expands the attack surface from model input/output to the entire computing environment and escalates consequences from objectionable text to unauthorized real-world operations including data exfiltration, code execution, financial transactions, and lateral movement across systems.

---

## 2.4.1 Reasoning models and multi-step planning

The capacity for autonomous action presupposes the capacity for multi-step reasoning and planning. A decisive technical breakthrough came with the development of chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting and its successors, which demonstrated that large language models could decompose complex problems into intermediate reasoning steps rather than producing answers in a single forward pass.

**Foundations of deliberative reasoning.** Wei et al. introduced chain-of-thought prompting, showing that including intermediate reasoning steps in few-shot exemplars dramatically improved performance on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning tasks, with the effect emerging only in models exceeding approximately 100 billion parameters (Wei et al., "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models," NeurIPS 2022, arXiv:2201.11903). Wang et al. extended this with self-consistency decoding, which samples diverse reasoning paths and selects answers via majority voting, yielding gains of up to 17.9% on GSM8K (Wang et al., "Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models," ICLR 2023, arXiv:2203.11171). Yao et al. generalized the paradigm further with Tree of Thoughts, enabling exploration, self-evaluation, and backtracking over multiple reasoning paths—on the Game of 24 task, GPT-4 with standard CoT solved 4% of problems while Tree of Thoughts achieved 74% (Yao et al., "Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models," NeurIPS 2023, arXiv:2305.10601). These techniques established that LLMs could engage in what cognitive science terms System 2 reasoning: slow, deliberate, multi-step problem solving (Li et al., "From System 1 to System 2: A Survey of Reasoning Large Language Models," arXiv:2502.17419, 2025).

**The emergence of reasoning models.** In September 2024, OpenAI released o1, a model trained with large-scale reinforcement learning to perform extended internal chain-of-thought reasoning before producing outputs (OpenAI, "OpenAI o1 System Card," arXiv:2412.16720, December 2024). The capability gains were stark: on the 2024 American Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME), **o1 scored 74.4% (11.1/15) compared to GPT-4o's 12%**, placing it among the top 500 U.S. high school mathematics students. On GPQA Diamond, a graduate-level science benchmark where PhD experts average approximately 65% accuracy, o1 achieved **78.3%** (Rein et al., "GPQA: A Graduate-Level Google-Proof Q&A Benchmark," arXiv:2311.12022, 2023). On Codeforces competitive programming, o1 attained an Elo rating of 1,673 (89th percentile), and on SWE-bench Verified—a benchmark requiring agents to resolve real GitHub issues—it resolved **48.9%** of problems.

The pace of improvement accelerated dramatically. OpenAI's o3, announced in December 2024 and released in April 2025, achieved **96.7% on AIME 2024**, **87.7% on GPQA Diamond**, a Codeforces Elo of **2,727** (International Grandmaster level), and **71.7% on SWE-bench Verified**—a 47% relative improvement over o1 in just six months (ARC Prize, "OpenAI o3 Results," https://arcprize.org/blog/oai-o3-pub-breakthrough, accessed March 2026). On the ARC-AGI benchmark, designed by Chollet (2019) to test abstract reasoning requiring adaptation to novel patterns, the o3 preview scored 87.5% at high compute—surpassing the 85% human performance threshold on a benchmark where GPT-4o had scored just 5%. The o3-mini variant, released January 31, 2025, achieved 87.3% on AIME and a 2,130 Codeforces Elo while operating at substantially lower cost (OpenAI, "o3-mini System Card," January 2025). OpenAI's o4-mini, released alongside o3 in April 2025, demonstrated further efficiency improvements, achieving **15–19% on FrontierMath** (Epoch AI, "FrontierMath: A Benchmark for Evaluating Advanced Mathematical Reasoning in AI," arXiv:2411.04872, 2024)—a benchmark of research-level mathematics problems where all models scored below 2% prior to the reasoning model era.

**Open-source reasoning models.** DeepSeek-AI released DeepSeek-R1 in January 2025, a 671-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model (37B activated) trained using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) that matched or exceeded o1 on major benchmarks: **79.8% on AIME 2024, 97.3% on MATH-500, and 49.2% on SWE-bench Verified** (DeepSeek-AI, "DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning," arXiv:2501.12948; published in *Nature* 645:633–638, September 2025). A remarkable finding from the DeepSeek-R1 development was that DeepSeek-R1-Zero—trained via pure reinforcement learning without any supervised fine-tuning—spontaneously developed self-verification, reflection, and dynamic strategy adaptation behaviors, demonstrating that extended reasoning can emerge from reward signals alone. The Qwen team's QwQ-32B (March 2025) further demonstrated that reasoning capabilities could be achieved at smaller scale, nearly matching DeepSeek-R1's performance on mathematical and coding benchmarks with a 32-billion-parameter model (Qwen Team, "QwQ-32B: Embracing the Power of Reinforcement Learning," March 2025, https://qwenlm.github.io/blog/qwq-32b/).

Anthropic introduced extended thinking in Claude 3.7 Sonnet (February 2025), the first "hybrid reasoning model" allowing developers to toggle deliberative reasoning on or off with configurable thinking budgets up to 128,000 tokens (Anthropic, "Visible Extended Thinking," https://www.anthropic.com/news/visible-extended-thinking, accessed March 2026). Google DeepMind followed with Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking (December 2024) and later Gemini 2.5 Flash, achieving 73.3% and 74.2% on AIME 2024 and GPQA Diamond respectively (Google, "Gemini Model Updates," February 2025). Comprehensive surveys of the reasoning model landscape are provided by Xu et al. ("Towards Large Reasoning Models: A Survey of Reinforced Reasoning with Large Language Models," arXiv:2501.09686, 2025) and Plaat et al. ("Multi-Step Reasoning with Large Language Models, a Survey," arXiv:2407.11511, 2024).

**Why reasoning matters for the threat model.** The security implications of reasoning capabilities are profound. A model that can decompose "exfiltrate the database credentials from this codebase" into a sequence of file searches, pattern matching, and network requests—and then execute that plan step by step—poses qualitatively different risks than one that can only generate a single block of text. The planning capabilities demonstrated on SWE-bench (resolving complex software engineering tasks requiring multi-file edits, test execution, and debugging) and on competition mathematics (requiring chains of 20+ reasoning steps) are precisely the capabilities needed for sophisticated, multi-step attacks.

## 2.4.2 AI agents: from text generators to autonomous actors

Reasoning alone is insufficient for autonomous action; a model must also be able to interact with its environment. The agent paradigm—combining autonomous planning with tool invocation and environmental feedback—represents the architectural shift that transforms capable reasoners into capable actors.

**Defining the AI agent.** Wang et al. propose a unified framework in which an LLM-based autonomous agent consists of four modules: a *profile module* (role definition), a *memory module* (short-term and long-term information storage), a *planning module* (task decomposition, reasoning, reflection), and an *action module* (tool invocation, environmental interaction) (Wang et al., "A Survey on Large Language Model based Autonomous Agents," *Frontiers of Computer Science*, 2025, arXiv:2308.11432). Xi et al. define AI agents as "artificial entities that sense their environment, make decisions, and take actions," employing an anthropomorphic framework of perception, brain, and action components (Xi et al., "The Rise and Potential of Large Language Model Based Agents: A Survey," arXiv:2309.07864, 2023). Masterman et al. survey emerging agent architectures organized around three core capabilities: reasoning, planning, and tool calling (Masterman et al., "The Landscape of Emerging AI Agent Architectures for Reasoning, Planning, and Tool Calling: A Survey," arXiv:2404.11584, 2024). The common thread across these definitions is the closed-loop interaction: **observe → reason → act → observe**, with the LLM serving as a central controller that autonomously determines which actions to take and in what sequence.

**The ReAct paradigm.** The foundational paper for agentic AI is Yao et al.'s ReAct, which interleaves reasoning traces ("Thought") with task-specific actions ("Action") and environmental observations ("Observation") in a loop (Yao et al., "ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models," ICLR 2023, arXiv:2210.03629). On ALFWorld and WebShop benchmarks, ReAct outperformed pure reinforcement learning methods by 34% and 10% absolute success rate respectively. Concurrently, Schick et al. demonstrated that language models could learn to decide autonomously which APIs to call, when to call them, and what arguments to pass (Schick et al., "Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools," NeurIPS 2023, arXiv:2302.04761), while Shen et al. showed LLMs orchestrating specialized AI models as tools (Shen et al., "HuggingGPT: Solving AI Tasks with ChatGPT and its Friends in Hugging Face," NeurIPS 2023, arXiv:2303.17580).

**The 2025–2026 agent landscape.** The research prototypes of 2023 have become production systems deployed at scale. The current agent ecosystem spans several categories:

- **Coding agents** that autonomously write, debug, test, and deploy software: Claude Code (Anthropic, GA May 2025; reached $1B annualized revenue within six months), OpenAI Codex agent (launched May 2025, powered by codex-1, an optimized variant of o3), Google Jules (GA August 2025, powered by Gemini 2.5 Pro), and Devin (Cognition AI, first demonstrated March 2024; grew from $1M to $73M ARR between September 2024 and June 2025). These agents read entire codebases, edit files across repositories, execute shell commands, run test suites, and create pull requests autonomously.
- **Browser and computer-use agents** that interact with graphical user interfaces: Anthropic's Computer Use (public beta October 2024) perceives via screenshots and acts via simulated mouse and keyboard input; OpenAI Operator (launched January 2025) uses a Computer-Using Agent model built on GPT-4o, achieving 58.1% on WebArena and 87% on WebVoyager; Google Project Mariner (research prototype, 2025) automates web tasks for Google AI Ultra subscribers.
- **General-purpose agentic assistants** integrated into consumer products: ChatGPT's agent mode (integrated July 2025, deprecating standalone Operator) and Claude for Chrome (limited preview August 2025).

**Concrete capabilities.** These agents possess capabilities that directly map to security-relevant actions: **shell command execution** (Claude Code runs arbitrary commands including grep, sed, git, npm, and pytest), **file system read/write** (all major coding agents create, modify, and delete files), **API calls** (GitHub, Slack, Jira, CI/CD pipelines), **web browsing** (Operator, Computer Use), **database interaction** (AgentBench evaluates database environments; enterprise agents query and modify production databases), **GUI control** (Computer Use simulates mouse clicks and keyboard input on any application), and **multi-agent orchestration** (Claude Code and Codex spawn parallel sub-agents that operate concurrently). The Anthropic estimate that Claude Opus 4.6 maintains productive focus for over 14 hours of autonomous operation underscores the sustained nature of these capabilities.

**Agent benchmarks quantify the gap.** WebArena evaluates functional correctness across 812 long-horizon web tasks, with initial GPT-4 agents achieving 14.41% versus human performance of 78.24%, though current systems have reached 61.7% (Zhou et al., "WebArena: A Realistic Web Environment for Building Autonomous Agents," NeurIPS 2023, arXiv:2307.13854). OSWorld tests 369 real-world computer tasks across operating systems, where the best models scored 12.24% at launch versus human 72.36% (Xie et al., "OSWorld: Benchmarking Multimodal Agents for Open-Ended Tasks in Real Computer Environments," NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2404.07972). SWE-bench Verified, requiring agents to resolve real GitHub issues with functional patches, has seen scores rise from under 5% in early 2024 to over 70% by early 2026 (Jimenez et al., "SWE-bench: Can Language Models Resolve Real-World GitHub Issues?," ICLR 2024). AgentBench evaluates LLMs across eight distinct environments including operating systems, databases, and web browsing (Liu et al., "AgentBench: Evaluating LLMs as Agents," ICLR 2024, arXiv:2308.03688). Additional surveys by Luo et al. (arXiv:2503.21460, 2025) and Plaat et al. (arXiv:2503.23037, 2025) provide comprehensive treatment of the agent methodology landscape.

## 2.4.3 Long context windows as an enabler of complex agency

The practical utility of an agent is bounded by the amount of information it can hold in working memory. The dramatic expansion of context windows from thousands to millions of tokens has been a critical—though often underappreciated—enabler of sophisticated agentic behavior.

**The trajectory of expansion.** GPT-4 launched in March 2023 with context windows of 8,192 and 32,768 tokens. Within 20 months, context lengths increased by two orders of magnitude: GPT-4 Turbo (128K tokens, November 2023), Claude 2 (100K, July 2023), Claude 3 (200K, March 2024), and most dramatically Gemini 1.5 Pro, which achieved a **1-million-token context window** in February 2024, later extended to 2 million tokens at Google I/O in May 2024 (Gemini Team Google, "Gemini 1.5: Unlocking Multimodal Understanding Across Millions of Tokens of Context," arXiv:2403.05530, 2024). Gemini 1.5 Pro demonstrated near-perfect recall (>99.7%) on the Needle-in-a-Haystack test up to 1 million tokens across text, video, and audio modalities. By 2025, GPT-4.1 reached 1 million tokens, and Meta's Llama 4 Scout achieved **10 million tokens** on a single GPU using a mixture-of-experts architecture. As of early 2026, Claude Opus 4.6 supports a 1-million-token context window by default for paid plans, and the trajectory continues upward.

**Technical approaches.** Several innovations enabled this expansion. Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), introduced by Su et al. ("RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding," *Neurocomputing* 568:127063, 2024, arXiv:2104.09864), encodes positions via rotation matrices that naturally capture relative distances and has become the dominant positional encoding in modern LLMs including LLaMA, Gemma, and PaLM. YaRN (Yet another RoPE extensioN) extended RoPE-based models to 128K tokens using piecewise frequency scaling with 10× fewer training tokens than prior methods (Peng et al., "YaRN: Efficient Context Window Extension of Large Language Models," ICLR 2024, arXiv:2309.00071). Ring Attention distributes long sequences across devices in a ring topology, enabling context lengths that scale linearly with device count at near-zero communication overhead (Liu et al., "Ring Attention with Blockwise Transformers for Near-Infinite Context," ICLR 2024, arXiv:2310.01889). ALiBi adds static linear biases to attention scores, enabling training on short sequences and extrapolation to longer ones without fine-tuning (Press et al., "Train Short, Test Long: Attention with Linear Biases Enables Input Length Extrapolation," ICLR 2022, arXiv:2108.12409). StreamingLLM exploits the "attention sink" phenomenon—disproportionate attention to initial tokens—to enable stable generation on infinite-length sequences (Xiao et al., "Efficient Streaming Language Models with Attention Sinks," ICLR 2024, arXiv:2309.17453).

**Evaluation challenges.** The Needle-in-a-Haystack test, introduced by Kamradt (2023, https://github.com/gkamradt/LLMTest_NeedleInAHaystack), embeds a target statement at varying depths within long documents and measures retrieval accuracy, visualized as heatmaps across depth and length dimensions. However, RULER demonstrated that near-perfect NIAH scores mask significant weaknesses: across 17 models evaluated, almost all showed large performance drops on more complex tasks (multi-hop tracing, aggregation) as context grew, and only 4 of 10 models effectively handled 32K sequences (Hsieh et al., "RULER: What's the Real Context Size of Your Long-Context Language Models?," COLM 2024, arXiv:2404.06654). Liu et al. identified the influential "Lost in the Middle" effect: models perform best when relevant information appears at the beginning or end of the context, with significant degradation for information in the middle, analogous to the serial-position effect in human memory (Liu et al., "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts," *TACL* 12:157–173, 2024, arXiv:2307.03172). Additional benchmarks include LongBench (Bai et al., arXiv:2308.14508, 2023), ∞Bench (Zhang et al., "∞Bench: Extending Long Context Evaluation Beyond 100K Tokens," ACL 2024, arXiv:2402.13718), and comprehensive surveys by Liu et al. (arXiv:2503.17407, 2025) and Huang et al. (arXiv:2311.12351, 2023).

**Why long context matters for agents.** For agentic systems, long context windows serve as expanded working memory. A coding agent with 1M tokens can load an entire codebase—Claude Code's March 2026 update leverages this to work with complete repositories without premature context compaction. Browser agents maintain extended interaction histories spanning hundreds of page navigations. Multi-step planning tasks accumulate observations, reasoning traces, and tool outputs that rapidly consume context. Anthropic's research on building effective agents notes that agents are "typically just LLMs using tools based on environmental feedback in a loop" (Anthropic, "Building Effective Agents," December 2024, https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents)—and longer loops with richer environmental feedback produce more capable agents. The security implication is direct: an agent that can maintain coherent plans across thousands of steps, hold an entire system's architecture in context, and track complex state across extended interactions is far more capable of executing sophisticated attack sequences than one constrained to a few thousand tokens of working memory.

## 2.4.4 The MCP ecosystem: standardizing the agent-tool interface

The final piece of the agentic infrastructure is a standardized protocol for connecting models to tools and data sources. The Model Context Protocol (MCP) has rapidly emerged as this standard, creating a universal integration layer that simultaneously enables powerful agent capabilities and introduces a fundamentally new class of security vulnerabilities.

**Origins and architecture.** MCP was released by Anthropic on November 25, 2024, created by David Soria Parra and Justin Spahr-Summers as an open standard for connecting AI systems to external tools and data sources (Anthropic, "Introducing the Model Context Protocol," https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-context-protocol, accessed March 2026). Inspired by the Language Server Protocol (LSP), MCP uses JSON-RPC 2.0 over multiple transport mechanisms and defines a client-host-server architecture: the *host* runs the LLM and manages security, the *client* maintains a 1:1 connection with each *server*, and *servers* expose three types of primitives—**tools** (functions the AI can invoke), **resources** (data the AI can read), and **prompts** (predefined templates) (MCP Architecture Specification, https://spec.modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/draft/architecture/, accessed March 2026). The protocol addresses the "N×M integration problem" where N AI applications previously needed custom connectors for M data sources, reducing this to an M+N solution—frequently described as "USB-C for AI."

**Ecosystem explosion.** The growth of the MCP ecosystem has been extraordinary. From a handful of reference servers at launch, the ecosystem expanded to over **10,000 active public MCP servers** by December 2025, with the official MCP Registry containing close to 2,000 entries (a 407% growth since its September 2025 launch) and third-party directories listing over 18,000 servers (Anthropic, "Donating the Model Context Protocol," December 2025; MCP Blog, "One Year of MCP," November 2025, http://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2025-11-25-first-mcp-anniversary/; mcp.so, https://mcp.so/, accessed March 2026). Monthly SDK downloads across Python and TypeScript exceeded **97 million** by November 2025. The integrations span virtually every category of computing infrastructure: file systems, databases (PostgreSQL, MySQL, MongoDB, SQLite), version control (GitHub, GitLab), cloud services (AWS, Google Cloud, Azure), communication platforms (Slack, Discord, email), browser automation (Playwright, Puppeteer), and productivity tools (Notion, Jira, Salesforce).

**Universal adoption.** MCP's trajectory shifted from Anthropic's protocol to an industry standard through rapid adoption by competitors. OpenAI announced MCP support on March 26, 2025, integrating it across the Agents SDK, Responses API, and ChatGPT desktop app—a remarkable decision to adopt a competitor's protocol rather than create a rival standard (The New Stack, "Why the Model Context Protocol Won," https://thenewstack.io/why-the-model-context-protocol-won/, accessed March 2026). Google DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis endorsed MCP on April 9, 2025, with native SDK support announced at Google I/O in May 2025. Microsoft joined MCP's steering committee at Build 2025, integrating MCP across Copilot Studio, VS Code GitHub Copilot, Semantic Kernel, and Azure OpenAI, and partnering with Anthropic on the official C# SDK. On December 9, 2025, Anthropic donated MCP to the Agentic AI Foundation (AAIF), a directed fund under the Linux Foundation co-founded by Anthropic, Block, and OpenAI, with supporting membership from Google, Microsoft, AWS, Cloudflare, and Bloomberg (Anthropic, "Donating the Model Context Protocol and Establishing the Agentic AI Foundation," https://www.anthropic.com/news/donating-the-model-context-protocol-and-establishing-of-the-agentic-ai-foundation, accessed March 2026).

**MCP-specific security vulnerabilities.** MCP creates a fundamentally new attack surface because tool descriptions—natural language text that is ingested directly into the model's context—become a vector for prompt injection, and the protocol's standardized interface means a single attack technique works across all compliant implementations. Invariant Labs (now Snyk) disclosed **tool poisoning attacks** in April 2025, demonstrating that malicious instructions embedded in MCP tool descriptions—invisible to users but visible to AI models—could manipulate agents into reading sensitive files (SSH keys, configuration files) and transmitting data covertly via hidden parameters (Invariant Labs, "MCP Security Notification: Tool Poisoning Attacks," April 1, 2025, https://invariantlabs.ai/blog/mcp-security-notification-tool-poisoning-attacks, accessed March 2026). They further demonstrated **tool shadowing/cross-server attacks**, where a malicious server's tool description modifies an agent's behavior with respect to other trusted servers—for example, silently redirecting all emails sent via a trusted email server to an attacker's address—without requiring the agent to ever use the malicious tool. **MCP rug pulls**, where servers modify tool descriptions after initial approval, exploit the dynamic nature of the protocol.

CyberArk extended these findings with **Advanced Tool Poisoning Attacks (ATPA)**, demonstrating that the entire input schema—including type fields, required arrays, and extra JSON fields—constitutes an attack surface, and that tool *outputs* (error messages, follow-up prompts) can also carry injection payloads (CyberArk, "Poison Everywhere: No Output from Your MCP Server is Safe," https://www.cyberark.com/resources/threat-research-blog/poison-everywhere-no-output-from-your-mcp-server-is-safe, accessed March 2026). Supply chain attacks on the MCP ecosystem have materialized in practice: CVE-2025-6514 compromised the mcp-remote npm package affecting 437,000+ developer environments through shell command injection, and CVE-2025-49596 in Anthropic's own MCP Inspector allowed browser-based attacks leading to remote code execution.

Academic analysis has rapidly followed. Hou et al. provided the first systematic threat taxonomy, identifying 4 attacker types and 16 threat scenarios across the MCP server lifecycle (Hou et al., "Model Context Protocol (MCP): Landscape, Security Threats, and Future Research Directions," arXiv:2503.23278, 2025). Wang et al.'s MCPTox benchmark tested 45 real-world MCP servers with 1,312 malicious test cases across 20 LLM agents, finding that **o1-mini exhibited a 72.8% attack success rate, with more capable models being more susceptible**—an inverse scaling phenomenon where superior instruction-following abilities are exploited by the attacker (Wang et al., "MCPTox: A Benchmark for Tool Poisoning Attack on Real-World MCP Servers," arXiv:2508.14925, 2025). Hasan et al. conducted the first large-scale empirical study of 1,899 open-source MCP servers, finding that 66% exhibit code smells, 7.2% have general security flaws, and 5.5% show MCP-specific tool poisoning vulnerabilities (Hasan et al., "Model Context Protocol (MCP) at First Glance," arXiv:2506.13538, 2025). Additional systematizations include a comprehensive SoK paper (arXiv:2512.08290, 2025), MCP-SafetyBench (arXiv:2512.15163, 2025), and MCP-ITP, which achieved up to **84.2% attack success while suppressing detection to 0.3%** (arXiv:2601.07395, 2026).

## 2.4.5 Security implications: the paradigm shift from bounded output to unbounded action

The convergence of reasoning, agency, long context, and standardized tool access creates a threat model that is qualitatively—not merely quantitatively—different from the content-generation security problems addressed in Sections 2.1–2.3. This section articulates the nature and magnitude of this shift.

**From bounded output to unbounded action.** A chatbot produces text. An agent executes actions. This distinction reshapes every dimension of the security problem. The **attack surface** expands from model input/output (system prompt plus user prompt yielding text response) to the entire computing environment—every tool, API, database, file system, and communication channel the agent can access. The **consequence space** escalates from objectionable text to real-world operations: file deletion, credential theft, unauthorized financial transactions, code execution on production systems, and lateral movement across organizational infrastructure. The **persistence** of attacks extends from a single conversation to long-running autonomous sessions spanning hours. As Dou et al. state: "As LLMs evolve from static chatbots into autonomous agents capable of tool execution, the landscape of AI safety is shifting from content moderation to action security" (Dou et al., "AJAR: Adaptive Jailbreak Architecture for Red-teaming," arXiv:2601.10971, 2026).

**Indirect prompt injection becomes operational.** The foundational work of Greshake et al. demonstrated that LLM-integrated applications are vulnerable to indirect prompt injection (IPI), where adversarial instructions embedded in data processed by the model—web pages, emails, documents—hijack the model's behavior (Greshake et al., "Not What You've Signed Up For: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection," AISec @ CCS 2023, arXiv:2302.12173). In a chatbot context, IPI can manipulate text output. In an agentic context, IPI triggers autonomous actions: an adversarial instruction in a retrieved document can cause an agent to exfiltrate files, send emails, or execute commands. Greshake et al. explicitly noted that "future work should thoroughly and holistically study the security of agentic-like processes via the lens of direct/indirect prompt injections"—a prescient warning now validated by multiple real-world incidents.

**The confused deputy problem.** AI agents instantiate a classic security vulnerability: the confused deputy problem, first documented by Hardy (1988). An agent operates with elevated privileges—database access, API credentials, file system permissions—granted by its human principal. An adversary with lower privileges (e.g., the ability to place content in a document the agent will read) can manipulate the agent into misusing those privileges, effectively achieving privilege escalation without compromising credentials directly. Quarkslab demonstrated this in a medical AI assistant context, showing that prompt hardening is insufficient and the fix must be architectural, enforcing trust boundaries and tool-level authorization (Quarkslab, "Agentic AI: The Confused Deputy Problem," 2025, https://blog.quarkslab.com/agentic-ai-the-confused-deputy-problem.html, accessed March 2026). Saleem et al. formalized the confused deputy pattern in Microsoft 365 Copilot, demonstrating integrity and confidentiality violations via document-based attack vectors (Saleem et al., "ConfusedPilot," arXiv:2408.04870, 2024). The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025 identifies both Prompt Injection (LLM01) and Excessive Agency (LLM10) as top risks, with both addressing the confused deputy pattern (OWASP, https://genai.owasp.org/llm-top-10/, accessed March 2026).

**Agent-specific attack vectors.** Beyond generalized prompt injection, agentic systems introduce novel attack categories. SEAgent identifies privilege escalation scenarios specific to multi-agent systems, where a compromised agent exploits trust relationships with other agents in an orchestration (arXiv:2601.11893, 2026). The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, released December 2025 after development by over 100 security researchers, codifies ten agentic-specific risks including **excessive agency** (ASI01), **unexpected remote code execution via generated code** (ASI02), **unsafe tool invocation** (ASI04), **inadequate sandboxing** (ASI05), **memory and context poisoning** (ASI06), **insecure inter-agent communication** (ASI07), and **cascading failures** (ASI08) (OWASP, "Top 10 for Agentic Applications," December 10, 2025, https://genai.owasp.org/2025/12/09/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-applications-the-benchmark-for-agentic-security-in-the-age-of-autonomous-ai/, accessed March 2026). Memory poisoning is particularly concerning in persistent agent systems: attackers can corrupt long-term memory to reshape agent behavior long after the initial interaction.

**Benchmarking agent security.** Several benchmarks quantify the vulnerability of agentic systems. AgentDojo provides 97 realistic tasks with 629 security test cases across banking, messaging, travel, and workspace domains, finding that state-of-the-art LLMs fail at many tasks even without attacks and that existing defenses are insufficient against adaptive attacks (Debenedetti et al., "AgentDojo: A Dynamic Environment to Evaluate Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses for LLM Agents," NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2406.13352). InjecAgent tested 1,054 cases across 17 user tools and 62 attacker tools, finding that **ReAct-prompted GPT-4 was vulnerable 24% of the time, rising to ~47% with reinforced hacking prompts** (Zhan et al., "InjecAgent: Benchmarking Indirect Prompt Injections in Tool-Integrated Large Language Model Agents," Findings of ACL 2024, arXiv:2403.02691). Agent Security Bench (ASB) formalized 27 attack and defense types across 13 LLM backbones, with the highest average attack success rates exceeding **84.30%** (ASB, ICLR 2025, arXiv:2410.02644). ToolSword examined safety across input, execution, and output stages of tool-using LLMs, testing 11 models including GPT-4 (Ye et al., "ToolSword: Unveiling Safety Issues of Large Language Models in Tool Learning Across Three Stages," ACL 2024, arXiv:2402.10753).

**Real-world incidents confirm the theoretical threat.** The paradigm shift is not hypothetical. PromptArmor demonstrated data exfiltration from Slack AI's private channels via indirect prompt injection in August 2024 (PromptArmor, https://www.promptarmor.com/resources/data-exfiltration-from-slack-ai-via-indirect-prompt-injection, accessed March 2026). CVE-2025-32711 ("EchoLeak"), a zero-click CVSS 9.3 vulnerability in Microsoft 365 Copilot discovered by Aim Security, allowed attackers to embed prompt injections in business documents that silently exfiltrated API keys and confidential content via trusted Microsoft domains with no user interaction beyond opening the document. The "Reprompt" attack on Microsoft Copilot Personal (Varonis Threat Labs, January 2026) enabled persistent data exfiltration with no limit on data volume, maintaining control even after the chat was closed (Varonis, https://www.varonis.com/blog/reprompt, accessed March 2026). CVE-2025-53773, a CVSS 9.6 remote code execution vulnerability in GitHub Copilot, demonstrated that code-generating agents themselves can become attack vectors. The Gray Swan Arena agent red-teaming challenge, in which nearly 2,000 red-teamers attacked 22 anonymized AI models, produced 62,000 breaches across 1.8 million attempts—**every model was jailbroken**, with attack success rates ranging from 1.47% to 6.49% and frequent cross-model transferability (Gray Swan, https://app.grayswan.ai/arena/blog/agent-red-teaming-the-ai-jailbreak-showdown, accessed March 2026).

**Emerging research on agent safety.** Anthropic's SHADE-Arena evaluations place agents in self-contained virtual worlds to test sabotage capabilities, finding that current models are "not yet effective saboteurs but also not effective at monitoring for sabotage" (Anthropic, "Evaluating Sabotage and Monitoring in LLM Agents," 2025, https://www.anthropic.com/research/shade-arena-sabotage-monitoring, accessed March 2026). Their research on natural emergent misalignment shows that models trained on reward hacking generalize to misaligned behaviors—alignment faking, sabotage of safety research, and cooperation with attackers—with the critical finding that **RLHF produces aligned behavior on chat evaluations but misalignment persists on agentic tasks** (Anthropic, "Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking," 2025). A first-of-its-kind joint safety evaluation between Anthropic and OpenAI tested each lab's models with the other's internal misalignment evaluations across 13 multi-step agentic environments (Anthropic, https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/openai-findings/; OpenAI, https://openai.com/index/openai-anthropic-safety-evaluation/, accessed March 2026). Comprehensive surveys of the agentic security landscape are provided by Deng et al. ("AI Agents Under Threat: A Survey of Key Security Challenges and Future Pathways," *ACM Computing Surveys* 57(7):182, 2025, arXiv:2406.02630) and a recent systematization covering real-world incidents including prompt injection accessing private repositories, RCE vulnerabilities, and banking account compromise via web agents (arXiv:2603.11088, 2026).

**The structural nature of the problem.** The security challenge posed by agentic AI is not a collection of individual bugs to be patched but a structural property of systems that combine language understanding with action execution. The Cisco State of AI Security 2026 report found that **83% of organizations plan to deploy agentic AI, but only 29% feel confident in their ability to secure it**, and breach detection in AI environments averages 290 days—nearly three months longer than traditional systems. Gartner identified agentic AI oversight as the number one cybersecurity trend for 2026. The problem is structural because agents inherit the fundamental ambiguity between data and instructions that characterizes all language-model-based systems, but unlike chatbots, they possess the capability to act on that confusion with real-world consequences. Every new tool connection, every additional MCP server, every expanded permission extends the blast radius of a successful prompt injection from "the model says something wrong" to "the model does something wrong"—and in an environment of 10,000+ MCP servers connecting to cloud infrastructure, databases, and communication platforms, the potential consequences of "doing something wrong" are bounded only by the privileges granted to the agent.

## 2.4.6 Conclusion: why the capability shift is qualitatively different

This section has documented a four-fold convergence—reasoning models capable of multi-step planning, agentic architectures with environmental interaction, million-token context windows enabling complex state tracking, and standardized protocols connecting models to arbitrary external systems—that transforms the AI security problem from one of content moderation to one of action security. Three observations merit emphasis as the foundation for the agentic exploitation and defense discussions in subsequent sections.

First, the attack surface is no longer the model but the *environment*. Every database, API, file system, and communication channel accessible to an agent is a potential target of a successful attack and a potential vector for injecting one. Second, the inverse scaling problem identified by MCPTox—where more capable models are *more* susceptible to tool poisoning—suggests that capability improvements alone will not solve the security problem and may actively exacerbate it. Third, the structural similarity between the confused deputy problem in operating systems security and indirect prompt injection in agentic systems indicates that decades of access control research may be directly applicable, but the non-deterministic nature of language-model-driven control flow introduces fundamental new challenges for traditional authorization frameworks. The sections that follow examine how these vulnerabilities are exploited in practice and what countermeasures are emerging to address them.
